usfm: PSA.139
title: "Psalm 139: Fearfully and Wonderfully Made, and Nowhere Left to Hide"
shortAnswer: >-
  Psalm 139 is best known for one line about being fearfully and wonderfully made,
  and read whole it is a stranger poem than that suggests. Its subject is being
  unable to get away. God knows every thought before it is spoken; verse 5 says he
  hems the writer in behind and in front, which is a besieging word. Then five
  escape routes are tried — heaven, the grave, the sunrise, the far sea, the dark —
  and none of them works. The reason turns out to be verse 13: you cannot get
  outside someone who made you from the inside. And the psalm ends by asking that
  God search the writer himself, immediately after he has declared that he hates his
  enemies.
outline:
  - verses: "1-6"
    heading: Known completely, down to the unspoken sentence
    note: >-
      Sitting down, standing up, walking, lying down, and words before they reach the
      tongue. Verse 5 puts it physically: hemmed in behind and before, with a hand
      laid on him. Verse 6 admits the whole thing is more than he can take in.
  - verses: "7-12"
    heading: Five places to run, and none of them work
    note: >-
      Up to the heavens, down to Sheol, east on the wings of the dawn, west to the
      farthest sea, and finally into the dark. Each is answered the same way: you are
      there. The last one gets the best line — darkness is as light to you.
  - verses: "13-16"
    heading: The reason he cannot get outside God
    note: >-
      Because God was already inside. Formed his inmost being, knitted him together
      unseen, saw the unfinished body, and had his days written down before any of
      them happened. The being-known of verses 1 to 12 turns out to be authorship.
  - verses: "17-18"
    heading: What he makes of it
    note: >-
      Not fear but appreciation: God's thoughts towards him are precious and
      uncountable, more than the sand. And then the quietest line in the psalm — when
      I wake up, I am still with you.
  - verses: "19-22"
    heading: The turn almost nobody quotes
    note: >-
      "O God, that you would slay the wicked." Bloodthirsty men, people who misuse
      God's name, and then a declaration: I hate them with perfect hatred. This is
      the same psalm, four verses from the end.
  - verses: "23-24"
    heading: The searchlight turned around
    note: >-
      Having asked for his enemies to be dealt with, he asks God to search him
      instead — know my heart, test me, see if there is anything offensive in me,
      lead me in the way everlasting. The last word is about his own correction.
themes:
  - name: Being fully known is the psalm's problem before it is its comfort
    explanation: >-
      The first twelve verses are not soothing on their own terms. Verse 5's "you hem
      me in" translates a word used for laying siege to a city; verse 6 says the
      knowledge is too much for him. Then he tries five ways out — the sky, the
      grave, the far east, the far west, the dark — which is not what someone does
      when they are enjoying being known. The psalm is honest that total exposure
      feels like being cornered before it feels like being loved, and it does not
      hurry past that. What resolves it is not a change of feeling but a piece of
      information in verse 13.
  - name: 'Fearfully and wonderfully made is the middle of an argument'
    explanation: >-
      Verse 14 is usually met alone, where it works as a statement about human worth.
      In place it is doing a job. The writer has just spent six verses failing to
      escape God, and verses 13 to 16 explain why the attempt was hopeless: God was
      present at his assembly, worked on his inmost parts, watched the unformed body,
      and wrote his days down in advance. So "fearfully and wonderfully made" is the
      reason there is nowhere to run. It is a claim about God's involvement rather
      than a compliment about the writer, which makes it sturdier — it does not
      depend on how good he happens to feel about himself.
  - name: It ends in hatred, and then in self-examination
    explanation: >-
      Verses 19 to 22 are shocking and they are deliberately placed. A psalm about
      being intimately known asks God to kill people and declares perfect hatred for
      them. Then, with no transition, verse 23 asks God to search the writer's own
      heart and see whether there is anything offensive in it. That sequence is the
      most instructive thing in the psalm. He has just aimed the searchlight at his
      enemies, and his final move is to turn it on himself — not withdrawing the
      prayer, but submitting the person who prayed it for inspection. It is what an
      honest person does after saying something like verse 22 out loud.
  - name: The comfort is that you cannot be lost, not that you are safe
    explanation: >-
      Nothing in this psalm promises protection. There are enemies in it at the end
      and they are still there when it finishes. What it promises is that no location
      and no condition puts you outside God's reach or notice — including the grave,
      including the dark, including sleep, since verse 18 says he wakes up still with
      God. For anyone who has felt unseen or unaccounted for, that is a different and
      often more useful comfort than a promise about circumstances. It works in
      places a promise of safety cannot follow you into.
glossary:
  - term: Sheol
    plain: >-
      The Old Testament's word for the place of the dead — not hell as later
      Christians came to describe it, but the shadowy realm everyone went to. Verse 8
      names it as the lowest place imaginable and says God is there too.
  - term: hem me in
    plain: >-
      The Hebrew word is used for surrounding a city under siege. It is not a gentle
      picture, and translations that soften it to something like "surround me" lose
      the pressure the writer clearly feels in verses 5 and 6.
  - term: fearfully
    plain: >-
      In verse 14 it does not mean nervously — it means in a way that produces awe.
      The older English sense is closer to "awesomely". The verse is saying the way he
      was made is startling, not that he was made anxiously.
  - term: the depths of the earth
    plain: >-
      A poetic way of describing somewhere hidden and unreachable, used in verse 15
      as a parallel for the womb. It is imagery for a place no human eye can see
      into, not a claim about where the writer was formed.
  - term: your book
    plain: >-
      A record kept by God, an image used several times in the Bible. Verse 16 says
      the writer's days were written in it before any of them happened, which is a
      statement about God's foreknowledge rather than a description of an actual
      volume.
  - term: perfect hatred
    plain: >-
      "Complete" or "whole" hatred — the word means without reservation rather than
      morally flawless. Verse 22 is saying his hatred holds nothing back, which is
      exactly what makes the next verse's request to be examined so pointed.
  - term: the way everlasting
    plain: >-
      The ancient or eternal way, contrasted with the "offensive way" in the same
      verse. It is the last phrase in the psalm and it asks for direction rather than
      reassurance.

caution: >-
  Verses 13 to 16 are the most quoted passage in Scripture in arguments about
  abortion, and it is worth being straight about what they do and do not settle. What
  the psalm says is clear enough: God was involved before birth, the unformed body
  was not hidden from him, and the writer's days were counted before any of them
  began. Christians on more than one side of the question take that seriously, and
  those who oppose abortion are not misreading it when they say the passage treats
  the unborn as known and intended by God. What the passage is not is a legal or
  philosophical definition. It is a prayer of astonishment, written in poetry, whose
  purpose in the psalm is to explain why the writer cannot escape God — not to fix a
  moment when personhood begins. Quoting it as though it closed the argument asks a
  poem to do the work of an argument, and quoting it as though it said nothing at all
  ignores what it plainly does say. The other misuse is smaller: verse 14 is not a
  verse about self-esteem, and treating it as one detaches it from the reason it is
  there.
faqs:
  - q: What does "fearfully and wonderfully made" mean?
    a: >-
      "Fearfully" here uses an older sense of the word, meaning in a way that
      produces awe — not anxiously. So the line means something like "I am made in a
      way that is startling and wonderful", and the credit is God's, not the
      writer's. It also has a job in the psalm. He has just tried five times to get
      away from God and failed, and verses 13 to 16 explain the failure: God was
      there at his making, working on his inmost parts, watching a body that was not
      finished yet. The verse is the reason there is nowhere to hide, which is why it
      does not depend on the reader feeling good about themselves.
  - q: What does "you knit me together in my mother's womb" mean?
    a: >-
      The Hebrew verb suggests weaving or interlacing, the kind of work that is done
      carefully and by hand, and the psalm pairs it with God forming his "inmost
      being" — the internal, unseen parts. Verse 15 doubles the image by saying he was
      made "in secret" and in "the depths of the earth", both ways of naming a place
      no human being can look into. Taken together, the claim is that God's
      involvement with this person started before anybody else knew they existed, and
      went all the way down rather than being a finishing touch.
  - q: Does Psalm 139 say life begins at conception?
    a: >-
      It does not use that language, and it is not trying to answer that question —
      but it says things that bear on it, and Christians disagree about how much
      weight they carry. What the text asserts is that God formed the writer's inner
      parts, that his unformed body was already visible to God, and that his days were
      written down before any of them happened. People who oppose abortion cite that
      fairly: the passage treats the unborn as known, intended and worked on. Others
      point out that it is a poem of praise rather than a definition, and that
      biblical poetry about God's care from the womb also appears in passages about
      calling and vocation rather than status. The honest summary is that Psalm 139
      is real evidence in the discussion and not a settlement of it.
  - q: What does "search me, O God, and know my heart" mean?
    a: >-
      It is an invitation to be examined, and its placement is everything. The writer
      has spent the whole psalm describing God's total knowledge of him, mostly as
      something he could not escape — and then, at the end, having just asked God to
      kill his enemies and said he hates them completely, he asks for that same
      searching attention to be turned on himself. So it is not a devotional
      flourish. It is a man submitting his own anger for inspection. The request has
      two halves: find anything offensive in me, and then lead me in the right
      direction. He asks to be corrected, not just examined.
  - q: What does "where can I flee from your presence" mean?
    a: >-
      It is a question asked by somebody who has thought about it seriously, and the
      answers are geographical: up to the heavens, down to the place of the dead, east
      as far as the sunrise, west to the furthest sea, and then into the dark. Each is
      the extreme limit of a direction, and each gets the same reply — you are there.
      Notice the tone. This is not a comfortable meditation on God being everywhere;
      it reads like somebody checking the exits. What changes it from claustrophobia
      to comfort is verse 10, where the hand that finds him in those places is
      described as guiding and holding rather than catching.
  - q: Why does Psalm 139:22 say "I hate them with complete hatred"?
    a: >-
      Because the writer means it, and the psalm does not soften it. Verses 19 to 22
      ask God to kill violent men who misuse his name, and the writer aligns himself
      completely with God's opposition to them — "perfect" here means whole or
      without reservation rather than morally admirable. Two things are worth
      holding. Jesus later explicitly tells his followers to love their enemies and
      pray for people who persecute them, so a Christian cannot simply adopt verse 22
      as an instruction. And the psalm itself supplies the corrective: the very next
      verse asks God to search the man who just said it and show him anything
      offensive in his own heart. The passage is a record of what a person prayed, and
      what he did straight afterwards.
  - q: What is the book in which all my days were written?
    a: >-
      An image, used elsewhere in the Bible, of God keeping a record. Verse 16 says
      the writer's days were in it before a single one of them arrived, which is a
      statement about God knowing his life whole rather than about the existence of a
      physical volume. Christians read the implications differently. Some take it as
      strong evidence that every event is determined in advance; others read it as
      God's complete foreknowledge without treating it as a script. The psalm does
      not adjudicate that, and its own use of the idea is simpler — it is one more
      reason the writer cannot get outside God's knowledge of him.
  - q: What does "if I make my bed in Sheol you are there" mean?
    a: >-
      Sheol is the Old Testament's word for the realm of the dead, where everyone was
      understood to go — not the later Christian picture of hell, and not a place of
      punishment in most Old Testament usage. Naming it alongside the heavens gives
      the writer the two furthest points on a vertical line: the highest place and
      the lowest. The claim is that God's presence has no upper or lower limit. It is
      also one of the more remarkable statements in the Old Testament, which usually
      describes Sheol as somewhere cut off from God's presence and praise. This verse
      says otherwise.

## If this is your first time reading Psalm 139

Read the whole thing, including the part about hating people. It is only
twenty-four verses, and the ending changes how the famous bit sounds.

The first half is not the warm poem it is usually quoted as. God knows when the
writer sits and stands, follows his path, and knows his sentences before he says
them. Verse 5 says God hems him in behind and in front, and the Hebrew word there is
the one used for putting a city under siege. Verse 6 admits the whole thing is too
much for him. And then verses 7 to 12 are a man checking the exits: the sky, the
grave, the sunrise, the far ocean, the dark. Every one comes back the same — you are
there.

That is the setup for the famous verse. "Fearfully and wonderfully made" is not a
compliment dropped into the middle of the psalm; it is the answer to why the escape
attempt failed. God did not learn about this man from outside. He assembled him. You
cannot get away from someone who was present at your making, and verse 16 pushes it
further: the days were written down before any of them happened.

Then read verses 19 to 22, which almost never get quoted. The same psalm asks God to
kill violent men and says, flatly, I hate them with complete hatred. It is jarring,
and it is meant to be there.

Because of what happens next. With no transition at all, verse 23 says: search me, O
God, and know my heart. See if there is any offensive way in me. He has just aimed
God's total knowledge at his enemies, and his last act is to point it at himself.

That is the shape of the psalm, and it is worth more than the nursery-wall version.
It is not about feeling good about how you were made. It is about a man who could not
get away from God, worked out why, and then asked to be examined.
