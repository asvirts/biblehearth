shortAnswer: >-
  Joshua 1 records the transfer of leadership after Moses' death. God commissions
  Joshua to lead Israel across the Jordan and repeatedly says, "Be strong and
  courageous." The third repetition asks, "have I not commanded you?" His courage
  will rest on God's presence and take shape through careful
  obedience to the Book of the Law. Joshua responds by preparing the people to cross,
  while the eastern tribes renew their promise to help the rest of Israel.

      work can continue through a leader with a different history and role.

faqs:
  - q: What does "be strong and courageous" mean?
    a: >-
      God commands Joshua to act faithfully in a frightening situation. The repeated
      command allows for fear while calling him to move forward. His confidence rests
      on God's presence, not on an inflated view of his own ability.
  - q: Does Joshua 1:8 promise success for reading the Bible daily?
    a: >-
      The verse tells Joshua to meditate "so that you may be careful to do everything
      written in it. For then you will prosper and succeed." This connects meditation
      with careful obedience and then with success in Joshua's assigned mission.
      Reading helps him remember and carry out God's
      covenant instructions. It supports the value of sustained attention to
      Scripture, but it does not guarantee that every personal plan will prosper.
  - q: What does "meditate on the law day and night" mean?
    a: >-
      The Hebrew word can describe softly repeating words. Joshua is to speak,
      remember, and continually consider God's instruction so that it directs his
      actions. "Day and night" describes an ongoing practice instead of a fixed daily
      schedule.
  - q: What does "I will never leave you nor forsake you" mean in Joshua 1?
    a: >-
      God assures Joshua that the divine presence Moses knew will continue with him
      in a specific leadership task. The promise does not make the coming conflict
      easy. Hebrews later quotes it when teaching ordinary Christians about contentment,
      giving the sentence a wider biblical application.
  - q: What does "every place your foot treads I have given you" mean?
    a: >-
      God grants Israel specific territory, with its borders described in verse 4.
      Walking through the land expresses possession of what God has already promised.
      The statement combines gift and responsibility within Israel's campaign; it is
      not a universal claim to any property a believer enters.
  - q: Why does God tell a military leader to read instead of giving him a battle plan?
    a: >-
      The commissioning establishes covenant obedience as the foundation of Joshua's
      leadership. Later victories and failures in the book turn on Israel's response
      to God's commands as well as military action. The absence of tactical advice in
      this speech keeps attention on the relationship that will govern the campaign.
  - q: Why do the tribes east of the Jordan have to fight for land they will not live in?
    a: >-
      Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh received territory east of the river after
      promising Moses that their soldiers would help the other tribes. Joshua holds
      them to that agreement. Their families and livestock remain behind while the
      fighting men serve until the whole nation receives rest.

## Reading Joshua 1 in its setting

Moses has died after leading Israel for 40 years. The next instruction is "now
therefore arise". Joshua stands beside a flooded
river with fortified cities beyond it and a nation waiting for direction. God's
first promise is continuity of presence: he will be with Joshua as he was with Moses.

The command to be strong and courageous appears three times from God and once from
the people. It calls Joshua to faithful action, while the repeated reason is God's
presence. His first response is practical and immediate: prepare the camp to cross in
three days.

God's instruction about the Book of the Law also deserves attention. Joshua must keep
it in his speech, thought, and conduct. The campaign will depend on covenant obedience,
a theme the rest of the book develops through both victory and failure.

The eastern tribes close the chapter by accepting responsibility for their neighbors.
Though their own land is settled, their soldiers will cross first and remain until
the rest of Israel receives a secure home.
