title: "Genesis 6: Two Hearts, One Verse Apart, and a Flood Caused by Violence"
shortAnswer: >-
  Genesis 6 starts the flood story, and its hinge is a pair of verses almost nobody
  reads together. Verse 5 says every inclination of the human heart was evil all the
  time. Verse 6 says the LORD was grieved in his heart. The same word, one verse
  apart — human hearts corrupt, God's heart hurting — and that is how the chapter
  frames the flood: not as cold judgment but as regret. The charge is named twice
  and it is specific: the earth was full of violence. The chapter also opens with
  four of the strangest verses in the Bible, about the sons of God and the Nephilim,
  and closes with a man who does exactly as he is told and never speaks a word.

  - verses: "14-17"

      God's grief — oddly calm. Then the flood is announced.
  - verses: "18-21"

      The first time the word covenant appears in the Bible. Noah, his wife, his three

themes:
  - name: Two hearts, one verse apart
    explanation: >-
      This is the best thing in the chapter and it is easy to miss. Verse 5 says the
      thoughts of the human heart were evil continually. Verse 6 says God was grieved
      in his heart. The same word, in consecutive sentences, applied first to people
      and then to God. Whatever else the flood is, Genesis refuses to present it as
      the act of a detached judge. It presents it as something done by someone whose
      heart is described in the same breath as the hearts that broke it. Read that
      pair and the chapter stops being a story about punishment and becomes a story
      about a project going wrong for its maker.
  - name: The charge is violence
    explanation: >-
      Ask most readers why the flood came and the answer will be something general
      about wickedness. The chapter is more specific than that, and it repeats itself
      to make sure: the earth was corrupt and full of violence in verse 11, God saw
      the corruption in verse 12, and in verse 13 God quotes it back as his reason —
      the earth is full of violence through them. The Hebrew word covers cruelty,
      oppression and the harm the strong do to the weak. What Genesis says destroyed
      the first world is not irreligion. It is people hurting each other, on a scale
      the earth itself is described as being full of.
  - name: Noah never speaks
    explanation: >-
      In twenty-two verses, Noah does not say a word. He is described, he is spoken
      to at length, and then verse 22 reports that he did everything precisely as
      commanded. There is no bargaining, unlike Abraham later on, and no protest,
      unlike Moses or Jeremiah. Whether that silence is meant as praise or simply as
      economy, the effect is a portrait built entirely out of compliance. The New
      Testament's summaries of Noah follow the same line: what is remembered about
      him is that he built the thing, for years, on the strength of weather nobody
      could see coming.
  - name: Regret is the word the Bible chooses
    explanation: >-
      Verse 6 says God regretted making humanity, and the sentence has troubled
      readers for as long as there have been readers. It sits awkwardly beside other
      passages saying God does not change his mind, and Christians resolve that in
      different ways — some reading it as language borrowed from human experience so
      that we can understand something otherwise unsayable, others taking it as a real
      description of God responding to what people actually did. What is not in doubt
      is that the writer chose it. He could have written that God was angry. He wrote
      that God was sorry, and grieved in his heart.
glossary:
  - term: the sons of God
    plain: >-
      The phrase in verse 2, and the Bible never explains it here. It is used
      elsewhere in the Old Testament for heavenly beings, which is one reading;
      others take it as the godly line of Seth, or as ruling dynasties. The choice
      changes what verses 1 to 4 are describing.
  - term: Nephilim
    plain: >-
      A word appearing in verse 4 and only once more in the whole Bible. Early Greek
      translators rendered it "giants", which is how it reached English Bibles, but
      the Hebrew probably means something nearer "fallen ones". Genesis never says
      who they were.
  - term: ark
    plain: >-
      The Hebrew word is not the usual one for a ship — it is used only here and for
      the basket Moses floats in as a baby. It describes a container built to survive
      water rather than a vessel built to sail, which is why it has no sail, no rudder
      and no mention of steering.
  - term: gopher wood
    plain: >-
      Nobody knows. The word appears once in the entire Bible and no ancient
      translator seems to have known it either. Cypress is the most common guess,
      because it resists rot and grew in the region.
  - term: cubit
    plain: >-
      An ancient measure taken from the forearm, roughly eighteen inches or
      forty-five centimetres, though it varied. It is why the ark's dimensions can only
      be given approximately.
  - term: pitch
    plain: >-
      A tar-like substance used to waterproof timber, applied inside and out. The
      Hebrew verb behind "coat it" is related to the word later used for making
      atonement — a coincidence of language that ancient readers noticed.
  - term: covenant
    plain: >-
      A binding agreement with obligations on both sides, more like a treaty than a
      contract. Verse 18 is the first time the word appears in the Bible, which makes
      its arrival here — in the middle of a flood announcement — worth noticing.
  - term: found favour
    plain: >-
      The Hebrew word is grace: Noah found grace in the LORD's eyes. It is the first
      time that word appears in the Bible too, and the order of the sentence matters —
      he finds favour in verse 8 before he is called righteous in verse 9.

caution: >-
  Verse 3 is regularly quoted as God capping human life at 120 years, and Genesis
  itself makes that difficult: Noah lives to 950, and Shem, Abraham and others after
  the flood pass 120 comfortably. The more likely reading is that the 120 years is a
  countdown — the time left before the flood — which fits the chapter it sits in. The
  bigger thing to be careful with is verse 6. It is used on one side to argue that God
  makes mistakes and changes his mind, and hurried past on the other as though it
  were only a figure of speech. Neither does it justice. Christians have long
  recognised a real tension between this verse and passages saying God does not
  change, and they answer it differently — some as language adapted to human
  understanding, others as a genuine account of God responding to human action within
  a plan he never lost hold of. What no honest reading can do is delete the sentence.
  The writer chose grief rather than anger, and that choice is the chapter's most
  important theological statement.
faqs:
  - q: Who are the sons of God in Genesis 6?
    a: >-
      Genesis does not say, and this is one of the genuinely open questions in the
      Old Testament. Three readings have serious support. The phrase is used elsewhere
      in the Hebrew Bible for heavenly beings, which would make verses 1 to 4
      describe something crossing a boundary between the spiritual and human worlds —
      the oldest reading, and the one the earliest Jewish interpreters took. Others
      read it as the godly line of Seth intermarrying with the line of Cain, which
      keeps the passage human. A third reading takes it as kings or rulers, who were
      sometimes given divine titles in the ancient Near East. The passage's
      difficulty is not a translation problem; the Hebrew is clear and the referent is
      not.
  - q: Who were the Nephilim?
    a: >-
      They are named in verse 4 and in only one other verse in the whole Bible, where
      Israelite spies report seeing them in Canaan. Genesis never defines them. The
      Greek translation rendered the word as "giants", which is how the idea of size
      entered the tradition and English Bibles, but the Hebrew more likely means
      something like "fallen ones". Whether they were the offspring of the unions in
      the same verse, a formidable people the verse merely dates, or simply the
      violent strongmen of a violent age depends on how you read one very compressed
      sentence — and the sentence itself says only that they were on the earth in
      those days, and afterwards too.
  - q: What does "his days shall be 120 years" mean in Genesis 6:3?
    a: >-
      Two main readings, and the second fits better. It could mean God is capping the
      human lifespan at 120 years — but Genesis then reports Noah living 950 years and
      several people after the flood living well past 120, which makes that hard to
      sustain. More likely it is a deadline: 120 years until the flood comes, a period
      of grace before judgment. That reading suits the immediate context, since the
      next thing the chapter does is describe God deciding to blot out what he made,
      and it explains why the number is attached to a statement about God's Spirit not
      contending with people forever.
  - q: How could God regret making humanity if he knows everything?
    a: >-
      It is a real tension and Christians answer it in more than one way. One approach
      takes the language as accommodation: Scripture regularly describes God with
      human words — hands, eyes, nostrils, and here a grieving heart — because there is
      no other vocabulary available to us, and "regret" conveys the reality of God's
      response without implying he was surprised. Another takes it more directly:
      God's plans are settled, but he genuinely responds to what people do, and this
      verse records a real reaction rather than a figure of speech. Both accept the
      point the verse is actually making, which is not about God's knowledge but about
      his heart. The flood is presented as something that hurt him.
  - q: How big was Noah's ark?
    a: >-
      Three hundred cubits long, fifty wide and thirty high, according to verse 15.
      Using a cubit of about eighteen inches, that is roughly 450 feet long, 75 feet
      wide and 45 feet high — about 137 by 23 by 14 metres. For scale, that is longer
      than a football pitch and comparable to a mid-sized cargo ship, and it would have
      been the largest wooden vessel in the world until the nineteenth century. The
      chapter also mentions three decks, a door in the side, and a roof, which
      together give a total volume in the region of one and a half million cubic feet.
  - q: What does it mean that Noah found favor in the eyes of the LORD?
    a: >-
      The Hebrew word translated favour is the word for grace, and this is the first
      time it appears in the Bible. Two things about the verse are worth noticing. It
      says he found favour, which is the language of receiving something rather than
      earning it. And it comes before verse 9 calls him righteous — the order runs
      grace first, then the description of his character. Readers have often taken
      that sequence as deliberate: Noah is not presented as a man good enough to
      deserve rescue, but as a man who was shown favour and whose life then matched it.
  - q: Was Noah sinless, or just righteous compared to his generation?
    a: >-
      The wording is careful. Verse 9 calls him righteous and blameless "in his
      generation", which reads more like a comparison than an absolute claim, and
      "blameless" in the Old Testament generally means whole or with integrity rather
      than without fault. Genesis itself settles the question a few chapters later,
      when Noah gets drunk after the flood in a scene the text records without excusing.
      So the answer is the second one. What the chapter credits him with is being the
      one man in a violent world who was going the other way, and then doing exactly
      what he was told.
  - q: What was gopher wood?
    a: >-
      Nobody knows. The word appears exactly once in the Bible, here in verse 14, and
      the ancient translators seem to have been as unsure as we are — several simply
      transliterated it rather than translating it. Cypress is the usual modern guess,
      because it grew in the region and resists rot, which is what you would want. Some
      have suggested it describes a treatment or a way of working the timber rather
      than a species. It is a good small example of how much of the Bible's practical
      vocabulary has simply been lost.

## If this is your first time reading Genesis 6

Read verses 5 and 6 as a pair, out loud if you can. They are the chapter.

Verse 5 is about the human heart: every inclination of the thoughts of it was
altogether evil, all the time. It is one of the bleakest sentences in the Bible and it
leaves no room at the edges.

Verse 6 is about God's heart: he regretted making man, and he was grieved in his
heart.

Two hearts, one verse apart, described with the same word. Whatever you have been
told about the flood, Genesis does not present it as a cold sentence handed down from
a distance. It presents it as something that hurt the person who did it. The writer
could have said God was angry. He said God was sorry.

Then look at the reason, because it is more specific than most retellings admit. The
earth was full of violence — stated in verse 11, seen by God in verse 12, and quoted
back by God as his reason in verse 13. Three times in three verses. Not irreligion,
not idol worship. Cruelty, and enough of it to fill a world.

Two other things worth knowing before you start.

The first four verses are famously difficult. The sons of God, the 120 years, the
Nephilim — Christians read all three in genuinely different ways, and the passage
does not explain itself. It is fine to read them, notice they are strange, and keep
going; the chapter's argument does not depend on settling them.

And watch Noah. He is spoken to at length, given a building spec down to the number
of decks, and told exactly who is coming with him. He does not say one word in the
entire chapter. The last verse is the only thing he does: he did everything precisely
as God had commanded him. For a man who spent years building a boat on dry land, that
is the whole portrait.
