shortAnswer: >-
  Genesis 6 begins the flood story by describing a world filled with corruption
  and violence. Human evil grieves God, who chooses Noah to build an ark and
  preserve his family and the animals. The chapter also contains a difficult
  passage about the sons of God, the Nephilim, and 120 years. Christians interpret
  those details in several ways, but the main story is clear: widespread violence
  brings judgment, and Noah responds to God's warning with obedience.

    note: >-
      The sons of God take wives from the daughters of humans, God mentions 120
      years, and the Nephilim are on the earth. One rendering says "in those days — and afterward as well".

  - verses: "5-7"
    heading: Human evil grieves God's heart
    note: >-
      Verse 5 describes the persistent evil of the human heart. Verse 6 says that
      the LORD regretted making humanity and felt grief in his heart. God's decision
      to judge the world comes from this sorrow over what people have become.
  - verses: "8-10"
    heading: Noah finds favor with God
    note: >-
      Noah "found favor" with the LORD. He is called righteous and blameless "in
      his generation," and he walked with God as Enoch did. These words commend
      Noah's life without claiming that he never sinned.
  - verses: "11-13"
    heading: Violence fills the earth
    note: >-
      The earth is corrupt and full of violence. God sees that corruption and names
      violence as the reason judgment is coming. Repetition gives the charge special
      weight.
  - verses: "14-17"
    heading: God gives Noah plans for the ark
    note: >-
      Noah is to use gopher wood and pitch, with a door, a roof, and three decks.
      The measured instructions prepare a vessel that can survive the flood God is
      about to send.
  - verses: "18-21"
    heading: God makes a covenant and names the passengers
    note: >-
      The word "covenant" appears here for the first time in the Bible. Noah's
      household, pairs of living creatures, and enough food for everyone will enter
      the ark.
  - verses: "22"
    heading: Noah obeys every instruction
    note: >-
      Noah does everything God commands. He never speaks in this chapter; his actions
      provide the entire response.
themes:
  - name: Human violence brings grief and judgment
    explanation: >-
      Verses 5 and 6 place the human heart beside God's heart. Human thoughts have
      become continually evil, and God is deeply grieved by what he sees. The flood
      is presented as moral judgment from a God who feels sorrow over the ruin of his
      creation.
  - name: The charge is violence
    explanation: >-
      The chapter moves from a broad description of wickedness to a specific charge.
      Verses 11 through 13 repeatedly say that the earth is corrupt and full of
      violence. The Hebrew word can include cruelty, oppression, and harm done by the
      strong to the weak. Human beings have made violence a defining feature of the
      world.
  - name: Noah never speaks
    explanation: >-
      Noah receives a long and demanding set of instructions without speaking. His
      portrait is built through action: verse 22 reports that he did everything God
      commanded. Later biblical summaries also remember the faith expressed in his
      preparation for a disaster he could not yet see.
  - name: Regret is the word the Bible chooses
    explanation: >-
      Verse 6 says God regretted making humanity. Some Christians understand this as
      human language that makes God's response understandable without implying that
      he lacked knowledge. Others take it as a direct description of God's real
      response to human choices. Both readings must account for the text's emphasis:
      humanity's violence brings God deep grief.
glossary:
  - term: the sons of God
    plain: >-
      Genesis does not identify them. Elsewhere in the Old Testament, this phrase
      can refer to heavenly beings. Other readers understand it as the godly line of
      Seth or as powerful ruling dynasties. Each view produces a different reading
      of verses 1 through 4.
  - term: Nephilim
    plain: >-
      This word appears in verse 4 and only once more in the Bible. Early Greek
      translators rendered it "giants", while the Hebrew may mean "fallen ones".
      Genesis does not explain who they were.
  - term: ark
    plain: >-
      The Hebrew word differs from the usual word for a ship and also describes the
      basket that carries the infant Moses. The ark is a container built to survive
      water, with no sail, rudder, or steering instructions.
  - term: gopher wood
    plain: >-
      The term appears only here, and its meaning has been lost. Cypress is a common
      suggestion because it grew in the region and resists rot.
  - term: cubit
    plain: >-
      An ancient unit based on the length of a forearm, often estimated at about 18
      inches or 45 centimeters. The exact length varied, so modern dimensions remain
      approximate.
  - term: pitch
    plain: >-
      A tar-like material used to waterproof timber. The Hebrew verb behind "coat
      it" is related to a word later used for atonement, a linguistic connection
      noticed by ancient readers.
  - term: covenant
    plain: >-
      A binding relationship established through promises and obligations, often
      closer to a treaty than a modern contract. Verse 18 is the Bible's first use
      of the word.
  - term: found favour
    plain: >-
      The wording "Noah, however, found favour." captures the turn in verse 8. The
      Hebrew word is also translated "grace." Noah receives favor in verse 8
      before verse 9 describes him as righteous, an order many readers consider
      significant.

  the number as the time remaining before the flood. Verse 6 also calls for care.

faqs:
  - q: Who are the sons of God in Genesis 6?
    a: >-
      Genesis leaves their identity open. The phrase can describe heavenly beings
      elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible, which supports the oldest Jewish interpretation
      of a boundary crossed between spiritual and human beings. A second view sees
      Seth's family intermarrying with Cain's, and a third sees kings or rulers who
      claimed divine titles. The Hebrew phrase is readable; the uncertainty concerns
      whom it names.
  - q: Who were the Nephilim?
    a: >-
      Genesis names the Nephilim here and once more when Israelite spies report seeing
      them in Canaan. It never defines them. The Greek translation calls them
      "giants," while the Hebrew may mean "fallen ones." Readers disagree over
      whether they were offspring of the unions in verse 4, a separate formidable
      people, or violent warriors. The sentence only says they lived on the earth in
      those days and afterward.
  - q: What does "his days shall be 120 years" mean in Genesis 6:3?
    a: >-
      The two main readings are a limit on human lifespan and a countdown to the
      flood. The lifespan view is difficult because Noah lives to 950 and later
      people also pass 120. A period of 120 years before judgment fits the immediate
      context and God's statement that his Spirit will not contend with humanity
      forever.
  - q: How could God regret making humanity if he knows everything?
    a: >-
      Christians give more than one answer. Some understand "regret" as language
      drawn from human experience so readers can grasp God's response without
      concluding that he was surprised. Others understand it as a direct account of
      God responding within purposes that remain secure. Both views take the grief
      seriously: the verse describes God's sorrow over what people have done.
  - q: How big was Noah's ark?
    a: >-
      Three hundred cubits long, fifty wide and thirty high, according to verse 15.
      With an 18-inch cubit, that is roughly 450 feet long, 75 feet wide, and 45 feet
      high, or about 137 by 23 by 14 meters. It would be longer than an American
      football field and comparable in length to a midsize cargo ship. The chapter
      also mentions three decks, a side door, and a roof.
  - q: What does it mean that Noah found favor in the eyes of the LORD?
    a: >-
      The Hebrew word translated "favor" is also the word for grace, appearing here
      for the first time in the Bible. Noah receives favor in verse 8 before he is
      called righteous in verse 9. Many readers see this order as significant: grace
      comes first, followed by the description of a life that reflects it.
  - q: Was Noah sinless, or just righteous compared to his generation?
    a: >-
      Genesis does not present Noah as sinless. The word "blameless" often means whole or
      marked by integrity, and the phrase "in his generation" places his life against
      the violence around him. His later drunkenness also shows that he remained
      fallible. This chapter commends his faithful direction and obedience.
  - q: What was gopher wood?
    a: >-
      Its identity is unknown. The Hebrew word appears only in verse 14, and several
      ancient translators simply carried it into their own languages. Cypress is a
      common modern suggestion because it grew in the region and resists rot. The
      term may also describe a treatment or method of preparing timber instead of a
      tree species.

## Reading Genesis 6 as a whole

Verses 5 and 6 explain the emotional and moral center of the chapter. Human thought
has become persistently evil, and God is grieved in his heart. The flood begins as a
response to a creation damaged by its human inhabitants.

Verses 11 through 13 sharpen the accusation by repeating the word "violence." The
problem includes cruelty, oppression, and the harm people inflict on one another.
The repetition keeps that human suffering in view when the judgment is announced.

The opening four verses remain difficult. The sons of God, the 120 years, and the
Nephilim have produced several serious interpretations, and Genesis provides too
little information to settle every question. The flood narrative can still be
followed without choosing among those views.

Noah's response is much simpler. God gives him exact dimensions, a passenger list,
and instructions for food. Noah says nothing in the chapter. Verse 22 closes the
scene by reporting that he carried out everything God commanded.
