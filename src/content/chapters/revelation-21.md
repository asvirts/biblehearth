usfm: REV.21
title: "Revelation 21: The City Comes Down, and It Is Shaped Like the Holy of Holies"
shortAnswer: >-
  Revelation 21 is where the Bible's story ends, and two details in it undo the
  picture most people carry. The direction of travel is downward — twice, in verses 2
  and 10, the new Jerusalem comes down out of heaven, and verse 3 says the point
  plainly: God's dwelling place is now with people. Nobody goes up. And when the city
  is measured, its length, width and height are equal. It is a cube, and the only
  other cube in Scripture is the innermost room of the temple, which is why verse 22
  says the city has no temple in it. Everyone now lives in the room one man used to
  enter once a year.
outline:
  - verses: "1-4"
    heading: Everything new, and one absence worth noticing
    note: >-
      A new heaven and a new earth, and "the sea was no more". Then the city descends,
      dressed like a bride, and a voice announces the headline: God is living with
      people. No more death, mourning, crying or pain — because the old things have
      gone.
  - verses: "5-8"
    heading: The one on the throne speaks, and it is not all comfort
    note: >-
      "Behold, I make all things new", then an instruction to write it down because it
      is reliable. Water for the thirsty, everything inherited by the one who
      overcomes — and then verse 8, a list of who is not in the city, headed by the
      cowardly.
  - verses: "9-14"
    heading: The tour begins with a wall and two sets of twelve
    note: >-
      An angel who was carrying a bowl of plagues offers to show him a bride. What he
      sees is a city: a high wall, twelve gates named for the tribes of Israel, three
      to each compass point, and twelve foundations named for the apostles.
  - verses: "15-17"
    heading: The measuring, and the shape it reveals
    note: >-
      A golden rod, and then the number that matters: length, width and height all
      equal, twelve thousand stadia each. The wall is 144 cubits. Nothing here is
      round; everything is twelves, and the city is a cube.
  - verses: "18-21"
    heading: The materials, and gold you can see through
    note: >-
      Jasper walls, a city of gold as clear as glass, twelve different stones in the
      foundations, and each gate a single pearl. The street is gold, and again
      transparent — a detail that keeps the whole description from being merely
      expensive.
  - verses: "22-23"
    heading: Two things missing that nobody would have expected
    note: >-
      No temple, because God and the Lamb are the temple. And no sun or moon, because
      the glory of God is the light and the Lamb is the lamp. The two great fixtures of
      ancient religion and of the sky are both simply unnecessary.
  - verses: "24-27"
    heading: Open gates, and one restriction
    note: >-
      Nations walking in its light, kings bringing their glory in, and gates that are
      never shut because there is no night to shut them against. Then the last verse:
      nothing unclean, nothing false, only those written in the Lamb's book.
themes:
  - name: The city comes down
    explanation: >-
      This is the detail that most changes how the chapter reads. Verse 2 has the new
      Jerusalem coming down out of heaven from God, and verse 10 says it again. Nobody
      in this chapter travels upwards. Verse 3 states the consequence in the plainest
      language in the book: the dwelling place of God is with man, and he will dwell
      with them. The popular Christian hope — souls departing to heaven — is not what
      Revelation 21 describes. It describes a renewed earth with God moving in, which
      is why the chapter is so physical: a city, a wall, gates, streets, stones,
      kings arriving with luggage.
  - name: It is a cube, and so was the Most Holy Place
    explanation: >-
      Verse 16 measures the city and gives its length, width and height as identical.
      That makes it a cube, which is a strange shape for a city and a very specific
      shape in the Bible: the only other one is the inner sanctuary of Israel's temple,
      the room the high priest entered once a year, alone, with blood. Read that way,
      verse 22 stops being a curiosity and becomes the obvious conclusion. There is no
      temple in the city because the city is the temple, scaled up to something like
      fourteen hundred miles on a side, with everyone living inside it. The most
      restricted space in the old arrangement has become the address.
  - name: The sea is gone, and that is not about oceans
    explanation: >-
      "The sea was no more" is easy to read as an odd loss. In Revelation the sea is
      not scenery. It is where the beast comes from, the standing biblical image for
      chaos and the deep, and — for a man writing this in exile on an island — the
      thing standing between him and everyone he loved. Its removal belongs with the
      removal of death, mourning, crying and pain in the next few verses: it is the
      end of separation and of the old threat, not a comment on the coastline. The
      same instinct is behind the gates never shutting. There is nothing left outside
      to shut them against.
  - name: The chapter is not all comfort
    explanation: >-
      Verses 4 and 5 are read at funerals and belong there. Verses 8 and 27 are read
      much less, and they are in the same chapter. One lists who has no part in the
      city and puts the cowardly first, ahead of murderers and liars. The other says
      nothing unclean or false will ever enter. Revelation is describing a place with
      a boundary, and the boundary is the point of the gates and the wall in a city
      that has no enemies left. A reading that takes verse 4 and drops verses 8 and
      27 has taken the consolation without the claim that makes it worth anything.
glossary:
  - term: the new Jerusalem
    plain: >-
      The city John sees coming down from God. It is described both as a bride and as
      a place, which is deliberate: it stands for God's people and for where they
      live at the same time. It is called new in contrast to the earthly Jerusalem,
      not as a rebuild of it.
  - term: the Lamb
    plain: >-
      Revelation's usual title for Jesus, used nearly thirty times in the book. It
      recalls the Passover lamb and the sacrifices of the temple. In this chapter the
      Lamb is both the city's husband and its lamp.
  - term: Alpha and Omega
    plain: >-
      The first and last letters of the Greek alphabet — the equivalent of saying "A
      to Z". The title claims that God is there at the start of everything and at the
      end of it, which is a fitting thing to say in the Bible's second-to-last
      chapter.
  - term: stadia
    plain: >-
      A Greek unit of distance, roughly 185 metres or 600 feet. Twelve thousand of
      them is around 1,400 miles or 2,200 kilometres — and it is twelve times a
      thousand, so the number is almost certainly symbolic before it is
      architectural.
  - term: the second death
    plain: >-
      Revelation's term for final separation from God, distinguished from ordinary
      death. Verse 8 identifies it with the lake of fire. The phrase implies that the
      first death is not the end of the story for anybody.
  - term: the Book of Life
    plain: >-
      A record of those who belong to God, an image used across the Bible from Exodus
      onwards. Verse 27 makes it the city's entry condition, which is a deliberately
      simple criterion after twenty-six verses of elaborate description.
  - term: the Most Holy Place
    plain: >-
      The innermost room of Israel's temple, where God's presence was understood to
      rest. It was a perfect cube, and only the high priest entered it, once a year.
      Knowing its shape is what makes verse 16 remarkable.

caution: >-
  Verse 4 is the verse most often read to grieving people, and it deserves to be —
  but read its own ending. There will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain
  "for the former things have passed away". It is a promise about a state of affairs
  that has not arrived. Used as though it applied now, it becomes a reason to tell
  somebody to stop crying, which is the opposite of what the verse describes God
  doing: wiping tears is an act that assumes there are tears. The other thing worth
  handling carefully is verse 8, and specifically the first word on its list. "The
  cowardly" heads a list that goes on to include murderers and idolaters, and it has
  frightened a great many people who are simply afraid. In a book written to
  churches under pressure to renounce their faith, cowardice means giving way under
  that pressure — abandoning Christ to stay safe — not being frightened. Revelation
  is addressed throughout to people who were scared, and it never treats fear itself
  as the disqualification.
faqs:
  - q: What is the new heaven and new earth?
    a: >-
      A renewed creation rather than a replacement for it. Verse 5 is precise: God says
      "I make all things new", not "I make all new things", and the Greek word for new
      here tends to mean fresh or renewed rather than newly invented. The picture in the
      chapter is physical and continuous with this world — a city, a street, gates,
      nations, kings bringing their glory in. The idea has roots in Isaiah, who
      promised new heavens and a new earth centuries earlier. What is gone is the old
      order of things: death, mourning, pain, and the sea. What remains is earth, put
      right, with God living on it.
  - q: What is the New Jerusalem?
    a: >-
      The city John sees descending from God, described both as a place and as a bride.
      That double description is intentional: elsewhere in the New Testament the church
      is called Christ's bride, so the city stands for God's people as much as for their
      address. Its gates carry the names of the twelve tribes of Israel and its
      foundations the names of the twelve apostles, which puts the whole history of
      God's people into the architecture. It is called new in contrast to the earthly
      Jerusalem rather than as a rebuilding of it, and crucially it arrives from above
      rather than being constructed from below.
  - q: What does "God will wipe away every tear" mean?
    a: >-
      It is a quotation from Isaiah, and the image is unexpectedly intimate: not the
      abolition of sorrow by decree but somebody reaching over and drying a face. Notice
      what it assumes. There are tears to wipe, and they are not treated as a failure —
      God is the one doing the wiping. The verse then lists what will be gone: death,
      mourning, crying and pain. And it gives the reason, which is also the timing: the
      former things have passed away. It is a promise about a world that has not arrived
      yet, which is why it comforts without requiring anybody to pretend they are not
      crying now.
  - q: How big is the New Jerusalem?
    a: >-
      Verse 16 gives it as twelve thousand stadia in length, width and height — around
      1,400 miles or 2,200 kilometres each way. Laid on a map that footprint would
      cover a substantial fraction of a continent, and the height is the same as the
      width, which no city has ever been. The wall is measured separately at 144
      cubits, roughly 216 feet or 65 metres. Both numbers are multiples of twelve
      (12,000, and 12 × 12), and twelve is the book's number for God's people, so the
      dimensions are almost certainly telling you something about completeness rather
      than giving you a survey.
  - q: Why is there no temple in the New Jerusalem?
    a: >-
      Because the city is one. Verse 16 measures it as a perfect cube, and the only
      other cube in the Bible is the Most Holy Place — the inner room of the temple,
      which the high priest entered alone, once a year. A temple exists to house God's
      presence and to regulate access to it, and both functions have become pointless:
      verse 3 says God now lives with his people, and verse 22 says God and the Lamb
      are the temple. So the answer verse 22 gives is not that worship has ended. It is
      that the building's whole purpose has been achieved so completely that the
      building would be in the way.
  - q: Are the streets of gold literal?
    a: >-
      Almost certainly not, and one detail in verse 21 shows why: the gold is described
      as clear as glass, which is not a property gold has. The same phrase is used of
      the city itself in verse 18. Revelation works by symbol throughout — a lamb with
      seven horns, a woman clothed with the sun — and this chapter's numbers are all
      loaded twelves. What the description conveys is value and transparency together:
      the most precious material anyone could name, with nothing hidden inside it. Read
      as a property listing it is oddly small. Read as an image it says something about
      the place that a literal pavement could not.
  - q: Why is there no sea in the new earth?
    a: >-
      Because in Revelation the sea is not scenery. It is the source the beast rises
      from, and the standing image in the Old Testament for chaos, danger and the
      untameable deep. It is also worth remembering where John was: exiled on Patmos,
      an island, with the sea between him and every church he was writing to. Its
      removal sits in the same list as the removal of death, mourning, crying and
      pain. The verse is announcing the end of chaos and of separation, not a change
      to the water cycle.
  - q: Who are the nations that walk by the city's light if everyone is saved?
    a: >-
      This is one of the genuine puzzles of the chapter. Verses 24 to 26 describe
      nations walking by the city's light and kings bringing their glory into it, which
      seems to assume people outside the city — and verse 27 has already excluded
      anyone unclean. Several readings exist. Many take the nations as the redeemed
      themselves, viewed in their variety rather than as a separate group, since
      Revelation elsewhere describes a saved multitude from every nation. Others read
      it as imagery borrowed from Isaiah, where foreign kings bring tribute to
      Jerusalem, carried over for its effect rather than its logic. A few read it as
      leaving room for something the chapter does not spell out. The text does not
      settle it, and it is worth holding lightly.

## If this is your first time reading Revelation 21

Revelation is a difficult book and this is one of its most approachable chapters, but
two things in it will probably not match what you are expecting.

The first is the direction. Verse 2 says the holy city comes down out of heaven from
God. Verse 10 says it again. Nobody in this chapter goes up anywhere. And verse 3
spells out what that means in the clearest sentence in the book: the dwelling place of
God is with man, and he will dwell with them. If you grew up with a picture of
Christian hope as souls leaving for heaven, Revelation 21 is describing the opposite
movement — heaven relocating here, to a renewed earth, permanently. It is why the
chapter is so full of solid things: walls, gates, a street, stones, kings arriving
with their luggage.

The second is the shape. In verse 16 an angel measures the city and the numbers come
back equal — length, width and height, all twelve thousand stadia. That makes it a
cube, which is a very odd shape for a city and a very particular shape in the Bible.
The only other cube in Scripture is the Most Holy Place, the inner room of the temple
that one man entered once a year, alone.

Hold those two facts together and verse 22 stops being a throwaway. There is no temple
in the city because the city *is* the temple, expanded to something like fourteen
hundred miles a side, with everybody living inside it. The most restricted room in the
old world has become the neighbourhood.

One last thing. When you reach verse 4 — no more death or mourning or crying or pain —
read the clause that finishes it, "for the former things have passed away". It is a
promise about a world that has not arrived. And notice the picture it opens with is
not tears abolished but tears wiped away, by hand, by God. That only makes sense if
somebody is still crying.
