shortAnswer: >-
  Psalm 91 expresses strong confidence in God's protection while naming dangers such
  as violence, disease, and hidden traps. The poem moves through three voices: a
  testimony in verses 1-2, words of encouragement in verses 3-13, and God's own speech
  in verses 14-16. God's closing promise includes rescue, an answer, and his presence:
  "I will be with him in trouble." The chapter offers refuge without turning faith into a guarantee that
  harm will never come.
outline:
  - verses: "1-2"
    heading: The worshiper makes God his dwelling
    note: >-
      "Dwell" and "abide" describe a settled life in God's shelter. The speaker then
      says, "I will say to the LORD, you are my refuge." He names the LORD as fortress
      and the God he trusts.
  - verses: "3-4"
    heading: God shelters people from hidden danger
    note: >-
      A second voice addresses "you" and names a hunter's trap and a deadly plague.
      God's protection is pictured through both the tenderness of covering wings and
      the strength of a shield and rampart.
  - verses: "5-8"
    heading: God's care reaches through every hour
    note: >-
      Night terror, daytime arrows, disease in darkness, and destruction at noon
      cover the whole day. The worshiper is told not to fear even when many people
      fall nearby, though the poem does not explain why some survive and others do not.
  - verses: "9-13"
    heading: Angels guard the path of the one who trusts God
    note: >-
      Verse 9 returns to the image of God as a dwelling place. The encouragement grows
      through images of angelic care and victory over lions and snakes. These lines
      remain part of the second speaker's address.
  - verses: "14-16"
    heading: God promises to answer and remain present
    note: >-
      God now speaks in the first person. He promises to deliver, protect, answer, stay
      with the worshiper in trouble, honor him, and satisfy him with long life. His
      presence within trouble becomes part of the promise.
themes:
  - name: The change in speakers helps define the promise
    explanation: >-
      Psalm 91 does not label its speakers, but its pronouns reveal three movements.
      Verses 1-2 give a testimony, verses 3-13 address the worshiper as "you," and
      verses 14-16 place "I" on God's lips. The broad declarations of protection sit
      in the encouragement section, including "No evil will befall you, no plague will
      approach your tent". God's closing words promise rescue, an answer,
      companionship in trouble, honor, and a full life. Listening to each voice keeps
      the poem's confidence and its realism together.
  - name: Refuge becomes a settled way of life
    explanation: >-
      Verse 1 uses "dwell" and "abide," and verse 9 again calls the LORD a dwelling
      place. These words describe an ongoing relationship with God. The shelter of the
      psalm is more like an address than an emergency stop. Its promises belong within
      a life of trust that continues before, during, and after a crisis.
  - name: Jesus shows how a true promise can be misused
    explanation: >-
      In Matthew 4, the devil quotes verses 11 and 12 while urging Jesus to jump from
      the temple. The quotation turns protection into a dare and omits the phrase "in
      all your ways." Jesus refuses to test God. This New Testament scene gives readers
      a clear boundary: trust receives God's care while walking faithfully, but it
      does not manufacture danger to force God to act.
  - name: The poem names fears with precision
    explanation: >-
      Hidden traps, plague, nighttime terror, arrows, midday disaster, lions, and
      snakes give fear a concrete shape. These dangers covered many of the threats an
      ancient traveler or community might face. Psalm 91 meets specific fears with
      specific images of shelter, making room for honest prayer about what feels
      dangerous.

      A raised defensive bank or wall around a fortress. Verse 4 compares God's

caution: >-
  Psalm 91 should never be treated as a contract guaranteeing that a faithful person
  will avoid illness, injury, violence, or death. God's closing words include "I will
  be with him in trouble," which assumes that trouble can arrive. Elsewhere in
  Scripture, faithful people become ill, suffer persecution, endure shipwreck, and
  die. Matthew 4 adds a direct warning about using verses 11 and 12 to justify a
  self-created danger: Jesus refuses the devil's invitation to jump from the temple
  and answers that God must not be tested. Trusting this psalm means seeking God's
  shelter in real danger. It does not mean taking an avoidable risk, refusing medical
  care, or blaming someone whose prayer did not end in physical safety.
faqs:
  - q: Does Psalm 91 promise Christians will never get sick or hurt?
    a: >-
      No. When God speaks at the end, he says, "I will be with him in trouble," so the
      promise includes his presence when hardship arrives. The rest of Scripture also
      shows faithful people becoming sick, injured, and persecuted. Psalm 91 gives
      strong assurance of God's care, rescue, and final security. It should not be used
      as a health guarantee, a reason to refuse wise precautions, or a basis for
      blaming someone who suffers.
  - q: What does "a thousand may fall at your side" mean?
    a: >-
      Verse 7 pictures mass danger, perhaps a battle or epidemic, while one person
      remains standing. It expresses confidence that God's care is personal even when
      suffering is widespread. The verse does not explain why one person survives and
      another dies, and it never suggests that those who fall deserved it. The poem
      leaves that painful question open instead of offering a formula for survival.
  - q: Why did Satan quote Psalm 91 when tempting Jesus?
    a: >-
      In Matthew 4 and Luke 4, the devil urges Jesus to jump from the temple and quotes
      the promise that angels will guard him. He leaves out "in all your ways," a
      phrase that places the protection along the path God has given. Jesus answers
      from Deuteronomy: "Do not put the Lord your God to the test." The exchange shows
      that biblical words can be quoted accurately and still be used wrongly. Trust
      follows God through real danger; it does not create danger to demand proof.
  - q: What is the fowler's snare in Psalm 91:3?
    a: >-
      A fowler caught birds with a concealed net or noose. The bird could enter the
      trap without seeing it, so the image represents danger hidden in advance. Verse
      3 pairs the snare with deadly disease, another threat that can arrive unseen.
      The picture acknowledges the limits of human awareness and asks God for care
      beyond what ordinary caution can provide.
  - q: What does "he will command his angels concerning you" mean?
    a: >-
      Verses 11 and 12 picture God directing angels to guard the worshiper along the
      way, even lifting the person over an ordinary stone. The phrase "in all your
      ways" connects this care with the path the person is already walking. The devil
      omits that phrase when quoting the passage to Jesus, changing guidance along a
      faithful path into supposed insurance for a reckless test.
  - q: What does "you will tread on the lion and the cobra" mean?
    a: >-
      Lions and snakes represent deadly threats in open country. Treading on them is a
      poetic image of victory after those threats have been defeated, not an
      instruction to approach dangerous animals. Romans 16:20 later uses similar
      language for God crushing Satan under the believers' feet. In Psalm 91, the
      image assures the worshiper that danger will not have the final victory.
  - q: Who wrote Psalm 91?
    a: >-
      The psalm is anonymous because its Hebrew heading names no author. Some early
      readers connected it with Moses because Psalm 90 carries his name. Others have
      suggested David, partly because a plague occurred during his reign. Neither view
      can be proven from the text, and the poem's message does not depend on choosing
      between them.

## If this is your first time reading Psalm 91

Pay attention to the pronouns as you read. They reveal three voices even though the
psalm never labels them. Verses 1 and 2 give one person's testimony about God.
Verses 3 through 13 address that person as "you." In the final three verses, God
speaks in the first person.

The shift matters because the middle voice makes sweeping declarations about safety.
When God speaks, he promises to answer, rescue, protect, honor, and stay *with* the
worshiper in trouble. Trouble remains part of the picture. God's own words ground the
chapter's confidence in his faithful presence instead of an untouched life.

The images of protection are intentionally vivid. Wings shelter the vulnerable,
angels guard the path, and dangerous animals end up underfoot. They invite frightened
people to bring concrete fears to God. They do not explain why faithful people
sometimes suffer, and they should never be turned into a promise that enough faith
will prevent illness, injury, or death.

Verses 11 and 12 later appear in the temptation of Jesus in Matthew 4. The devil uses
them to urge Jesus into a needless risk, and Jesus refuses to test God. That scene
offers a practical guide for reading Psalm 91: seek God as your shelter when danger
comes, and continue to use wisdom instead of creating danger to prove a promise.
