title: "Hebrews 11: The Hall of Faith Turns Over Halfway Through Verse 35"
seoTitle: "Hebrews 11: Where the Hall of Faith Turns"
shortAnswer: >-
  Hebrews 11 is known as the Hall of Faith, and the thing worth knowing about it is
  where the list breaks. Through verse 34 faith produces results you would want:
  kingdoms conquered, lions silenced, fire quenched, armies routed, the dead raised.
  Then, in the middle of verse 35, the sentence turns — "others were tortured and
  refused their release" — and the rest is mocking, flogging, chains, stoning, being
  sawn in two, and hiding in caves. Same faith, opposite outcomes, and the chapter
  refuses to rank them. Twice it says these people died without receiving what they
  were promised.
outline:
  - verses: "1-3"
    heading: A definition, and the first thing it is applied to
    note: >-
      Faith as the assurance of what is hoped for and the certainty of what is not
      seen. Then, before any human example, the universe itself: what is visible was
      not made out of what was visible. The chapter starts with the largest possible
      unseen thing.
  - verses: "4-7"
    heading: Three men from before the flood
    note: >-
      Abel, whose offering was accepted and who "still speaks" though dead. Enoch,
      who was taken and did not die. Noah, who built for a flood nobody could see
      coming. Between them, verse 6: without faith it is impossible to please God.
  - verses: "8-12"
    heading: Abraham and Sarah, leaving without directions
    note: >-
      Abraham obeys and goes without knowing where he is going, then lives in the
      promised land as a foreigner, in tents. Sarah conceives past the age for it.
      Verse 10 names what he was actually after: a city with foundations, built by
      God.
  - verses: "13-16"
    heading: The aside that gives the whole chapter away
    note: >-
      All these died in faith without having received what was promised — they saw
      it from a distance and waved. They admitted to being strangers and exiles, and
      that admission is treated as evidence they were after a different country
      altogether.
  - verses: "17-22"
    heading: Isaac on the altar, and three deathbeds
    note: >-
      Abraham prepared to give up the son the promise depended on, reasoning that
      God could raise the dead. Then Isaac, Jacob and Joseph, all shown blessing or
      giving instructions as they die — faith exercised with no time left to see
      results.
  - verses: "23-29"
    heading: Moses, making the worse choice on purpose
    note: >-
      Hidden as a baby by parents who ignored a royal order. Then a grown man
      turning down the Egyptian court, choosing ill-treatment with slaves over the
      treasures of an empire, and leaving because "he saw him who is invisible".
  - verses: "30-35"
    heading: Faith that wins, at speed
    note: >-
      Jericho's walls, Rahab, then a rush of names and results: kingdoms conquered,
      justice administered, promises obtained, lions' mouths shut, flames quenched,
      swords escaped, weakness turned to strength, armies routed, dead children
      handed back alive.
  - verses: "35-38"
    heading: And then the same faith, losing
    note: >-
      The sentence turns mid-verse. Others were tortured and turned down release.
      Mocked, flogged, chained, imprisoned, stoned, sawn in two, killed by the
      sword, wandering in animal skins, living in caves. The verdict on them is the
      chapter's highest: the world was not worthy of them.
  - verses: "39-40"
    heading: None of them got it, and that is the point
    note: >-
      All commended, none of them received what was promised — said for the second
      time. The reason given is startling: God had something better planned, and it
      involves the readers. They are waiting for us.
themes:
  - name: The list turns over in the middle of verse 35
    explanation: >-
      This is the chapter's most important feature and the one most often edited out
      in the retelling. Everything from verse 30 to the first half of verse 35 is
      faith with a happy ending — walls down, lions muzzled, fire survived, children
      raised. Then the same sentence continues: others were tortured and refused
      their release. What follows is a catalogue of people whose faith produced no
      rescue at all. The writer does not present the second group as a lesser tier
      or explain why the outcomes differed. He gives them the higher commendation:
      the world was not worthy of them. Any use of this chapter that stops at verse
      35a has removed half of what it says about faith.
  - name: Twice it says they never received what was promised
    explanation: >-
      Verse 13 and verse 39 make the same point, and they bracket the whole list.
      These people died without getting the thing they were promised; they saw it
      from a long way off and welcomed it anyway. That is a strange way to build a
      chapter about the power of faith, unless faith is not primarily a mechanism
      for obtaining things. On the chapter's own account, faith is what keeps people
      moving towards something they will not live to see, which is exactly what the
      definition in verse 1 describes — certainty about what is not visible. The
      list is not a set of case studies in getting results. It is a set of case
      studies in going without them.
  - name: It commends their faith, not their character
    explanation: >-
      Read the names carefully. Rahab is introduced with her profession attached.
      Jacob cheated his brother and his father-in-law. Samson's story is a
      catastrophe of appetite; Jephthah made a vow that destroyed his daughter;
      David's worst act is famous. Moses is here for refusing Egypt, not for
      anything about his temper. The writer is not holding up moral examples and he
      never claims to be. What each of these people did was take God at his word
      about something they could not see, and that is the only qualification the
      chapter checks. It is a considerable relief for anybody who has read the rest
      of their stories.
  - name: The definition is proved by people who never saw it
    explanation: >-
      Verse 1 defines faith in terms of two absences: what we hope for and do not
      yet have, and what we do not see. The forty verses that follow are the
      argument for that definition, and they make it by example rather than by
      logic. Abraham left without knowing the destination. Noah built for weather
      with no clouds in it. Moses walked away from an empire because he could see
      someone invisible. And the whole company died without the promise in hand. If
      faith were mainly about outcomes, this would be a chapter of failures. Because
      it is about a settled confidence in something unseen, it is a chapter of
      witnesses.
glossary:
  - term: faith
    plain: >-
      In verse 1 it has two parts: a solid confidence about something you are still
      hoping for, and a settled conviction about something you cannot see. Older
      Bibles say "substance" and "evidence", which sound abstract. The idea is
      closer to treating something you cannot yet verify as real enough to act on.
  - term: commended
    plain: >-
      Approved of, spoken well of, given a good testimony. The word appears at both
      ends of the chapter, in verses 2 and 39, and in both places it is God who does
      the commending — which is why it can sit next to the fact that these people
      got nothing they were promised.
  - term: strangers and exiles
    plain: >-
      People living somewhere that is not their own country, without the rights of
      locals. Verse 13 says these people admitted to being that, and the writer
      treats the admission as revealing: someone who says they do not belong here is
      implying they belong somewhere.
  - term: the city with foundations
    plain: >-
      What verse 10 says Abraham was actually after, in contrast to the tents he
      lived in. A city with foundations is permanent, and God is named as its
      architect and builder. Verse 16 calls the same thing a better country, a
      heavenly one.
  - term: made perfect
    plain: >-
      Brought to completion, finished — not made morally spotless. Verse 40 says the
      earlier believers are completed together with the chapter's readers, which
      means their story was left deliberately unfinished until something later
      happened.
  - term: Rahab
    plain: >-
      A woman in Jericho who hid Israelite spies before the city fell, and who is
      described in Joshua and here as a prostitute. She turns up again in Matthew's
      genealogy of Jesus. The chapter does not tidy up her description.

caution: >-
  This chapter is regularly quoted as evidence that faith produces results, and the
  quoting almost always stops in the middle of verse 35. Read to the end and the
  same faith that shut lions' mouths also belonged to people who were flogged,
  imprisoned, stoned, sawn in two and left living in caves — and the writer's only
  comment on that second group is that the world was not worthy of them. He does
  not explain the difference in outcome, and he does not suggest the rescued had
  more faith than the tortured. Any teaching that treats the first half of the list
  as the normal Christian experience and the second half as a failure of belief has
  inverted the chapter. The other line worth handling carefully is verse 6, "without
  faith it is impossible to please God", which gets used to frighten people who are
  struggling to believe. Its own second half defines what it means by faith:
  believing that God exists and that he rewards people who genuinely look for him.
  That is a low bar deliberately, and it is the bar every person in the list clears
  while getting almost everything else wrong.
faqs:
  - q: What does "faith is the substance of things hoped for" mean?
    a: >-
      Verse 1 has two halves and both point at something absent. The first word,
      rendered "substance" in older Bibles and "assurance" or "confidence" in
      newer ones, carries the idea of something standing under and holding up — so
      faith is what gives weight and solidity to a hope you have not yet collected.
      The second half says faith is the conviction or proof concerning things not
      seen. Put plainly: faith is treating something you cannot verify yet as real
      enough to build your life on. The rest of the chapter argues for that reading
      by example, and every example is someone acting on something invisible.
  - q: Who is in the Hebrews 11 hall of faith?
    a: >-
      Named: Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Moses and his
      parents, Rahab, then Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David and Samuel, plus
      "the prophets". After that the writer stops naming people and describes them
      by what happened to them — those who conquered kingdoms and shut lions'
      mouths, and then those who were tortured, stoned and sawn in two. He says
      explicitly in verse 32 that he is running out of time, so the list is
      deliberately incomplete.
  - q: Why does Hebrews 11:35 say some were delivered and others were tortured?
    a: >-
      Because both happened, and the writer refuses to pretend otherwise. The
      sentence begins with women receiving their dead back raised to life and
      continues, in the same verse, with others who were tortured and turned down
      the offer of release. He gives no explanation for the difference and makes no
      suggestion that the second group believed less. What he does do is reserve his
      strongest praise for them: the world was not worthy of them. That is the
      chapter's answer to the question, and it is not a mechanism. It is a refusal
      to treat outcome as the measure of faith.
  - q: What does "without faith it is impossible to please God" mean?
    a: >-
      The verse defines its own terms in the second half: anyone approaching God
      must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who earnestly seek him.
      That is what the faith in question consists of — not certainty about every
      doctrine, and not the absence of doubt. It is the minimum required to come at
      all, which is why it sits in the middle of a list of people who did come and
      then failed at a great many other things. If the verse is being used to tell
      you that struggling with belief puts you outside God's favour, notice that
      almost everybody named in this chapter would fail that harsher test.
  - q: Why is Rahab a prostitute listed as a hero of faith?
    a: >-
      Because the chapter is commending faith and not character, and it does not
      soften her description to make the point. Rahab hid Israelite spies in Jericho
      before the city fell, acting on what she had heard about their God against the
      interests of her own city — which is precisely the pattern the chapter is
      collecting. She is not the only awkward name on the list: Jacob was a cheat,
      Samson's life was a wreck, and Jephthah's vow destroyed his own daughter. The
      writer's silence about all of that is itself the message. What is being
      commended is that they took God at his word.
  - q: Who was sawn in two in Hebrews 11:37?
    a: >-
      The Bible does not say, and the writer clearly expected his readers to
      recognise the reference. Jewish tradition outside the Bible holds that the
      prophet Isaiah was executed this way by King Manasseh, and that is the usual
      identification. Several other items in verses 35 to 38 also point outside the
      Old Testament — people tortured who refused release closely matches accounts
      from the Maccabean period, a couple of centuries before Jesus. The writer is
      drawing on the whole memory of his people's martyrs, not only on Scripture,
      which is worth knowing when the names cannot be found.
  - q: What is the better country they were seeking?
    a: >-
      Verse 16 calls it a better country, a heavenly one, and verse 10 calls it a
      city with foundations whose architect and builder is God. The contrast is with
      what Abraham actually had: tents, in a land he was promised but lived in as a
      foreigner. The writer's argument in verses 13 to 16 is that people who
      describe themselves as strangers and exiles are admitting they have somewhere
      else in mind — and that they could have gone back to the country they left if
      that had been what they wanted. What they were after was permanent and not yet
      built.
  - q: How did Abraham reason that God could raise Isaac from the dead?
    a: >-
      Verse 19 says he reasoned it, which is unusual — the chapter normally reports
      what people did rather than what they worked out. The logic reconstructs
      easily. God had promised that Abraham's descendants would come through Isaac,
      and God was now asking for Isaac. Both things could not be true unless God
      intended to give Isaac back. So Abraham concluded that God could raise the
      dead, and went. The chapter adds that in a sense he did receive him back from
      death. It is one of the few places in the Bible where faith is shown as an
      argument being followed to its conclusion rather than a feeling being held on
      to.

## If this is your first time reading Hebrews 11

Read it all the way to verse 40, and pay particular attention to verse 35, because
the chapter turns over inside that one verse and most summaries of it do not.

Here is what happens. From verse 30 the writer picks up speed. Jericho's walls
collapse. Rahab survives. Then a rush: kingdoms conquered, justice done, promises
obtained, lions' mouths shut, fire quenched, swords dodged, weakness turned to
strength, armies put to flight, dead children handed back to their mothers alive.
It reads like a highlight reel, and if you have only ever heard this chapter
preached, this is probably the part you heard.

Then the same sentence keeps going. "Others were tortured and refused their
release." And from there: mocked, flogged, chained, imprisoned, stoned, sawn in two,
killed with the sword, walking around in animal skins, destitute, living in caves
and holes in the ground.

The writer offers no explanation for the difference. He does not say the first group
believed harder. What he does say, about the second group, is the highest thing he
says about anybody in the chapter: the world was not worthy of them.

And then he closes the whole list twice over with the same fact, once at verse 13
and again at verse 39 — none of these people received what they were promised. They
saw it from a distance and waved at it, and died.

Which sounds like a strange way to end a chapter about faith, until you go back to
verse 1. Faith there is defined as confidence about what you hope for and have not
got, and conviction about what you cannot see. On that definition, forty verses of
people who never saw it is not a list of failures. It is the proof.
