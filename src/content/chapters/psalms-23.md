usfm: PSA.23
title: "Psalm 23: The Lord Is My Shepherd, Explained Line by Line"
shortAnswer: >-
  Psalm 23 is a short song in which David describes God as a shepherd who feeds
  him, leads him, stays with him through danger and never runs out of kindness.
  Six verses, two pictures: God as a shepherd out in open country, then God as a
  host at a full table. Its subject is not a life without trouble — there is a
  dark valley and there are enemies — but the presence of someone who does not
  leave.
outline:
  - verses: "1"
    heading: The claim the whole psalm rests on
    note: >-
      "The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want." Everything else in the psalm
      explains what that sentence means in practice. Note "my" — this is a
      personal claim, not a general observation about God.
  - verses: "2-3"
    heading: The shepherd feeds, waters and restores
    note: >-
      Green grass, still water, and a soul brought back to life. Sheep will not
      drink from fast-moving water, so "still waters" is a shepherd's detail: God
      provides in the form the creature can actually receive.
  - verses: "4"
    heading: The valley, and the reason it is survivable
    note: >-
      The psalm walks straight into the worst place in it and the language
      changes — David stops talking about God and starts talking to him. The
      comfort offered is not rescue from the valley but company inside it.
  - verses: "5"
    heading: The picture changes from field to table
    note: >-
      Suddenly God is a host, not a shepherd: a laid table, oil poured on the
      head, a cup filled past the brim. And the enemies are still in the room.
      Honour is given in front of the people hoping to see him shamed.
  - verses: "6"
    heading: Goodness and mercy as pursuers
    note: >-
      The Hebrew verb is stronger than "follow" — it is the word used for
      chasing something down. David is not being trailed by God's kindness; he is
      being hunted by it, all his life, all the way home.
themes:
  - name: God as shepherd, not manager
    explanation: >-
      Shepherding in ancient Israel was unglamorous, physical, round-the-clock
      work. A shepherd walked with the flock, slept near it, and personally
      fought off whatever came. Calling God a shepherd is not a claim that God
      supervises from a distance; it is a claim that he is close by, awake, and
      willing to get involved.
  - name: Presence rather than protection
    explanation: >-
      The most quoted line in the psalm is about walking through the valley of
      the shadow of death, and it does not say the valley is avoided. It says "I
      will fear no evil, for you are with me". This is the psalm's central
      offer, and it is worth being honest that it is a smaller promise than many
      people want and a bigger one than they expect.
  - name: Enough, not excess
    explanation: >-
      "I shall not want" means "I will not lack" — not "I will get everything I
      ask for". A sheep with a good shepherd does not have a life free of
      weather, distance or predators. It has a life where nothing it genuinely
      needs is missing. The psalm is careful about that difference and it is
      easy to blur.
  - name: Honour in front of enemies
    explanation: >-
      Verse 5 does not remove the enemies. It seats David at a full table while
      they watch. In a culture where public shame was one of the worst things
      that could happen to a person, being honoured in front of people who
      wanted you humiliated was a specific and powerful form of vindication.
glossary:
  - term: The Lord
    plain: >-
      Where your Bible prints LORD in capitals, the Hebrew has God's personal
      name, the one revealed to Moses. So the first line is not "a god is my
      shepherd" but the name of a specific God whom David knows.
  - term: I shall not want
    plain: >-
      Older English for "I will not lack anything I need". "Want" here means to
      be short of something, not to desire it. It is a statement about supply,
      not about getting your wishes.
  - term: Restores my soul
    plain: >-
      Brings me back to life. The Hebrew word for soul means the whole living
      person, not an invisible part inside them, so this is closer to "he
      revives me" than to anything ghostly.
  - term: The valley of the shadow of death
    plain: >-
      A deep, dark ravine — the kind of narrow place where a flock is most
      exposed. The phrase can be translated "darkest valley". It covers dying,
      but it is not only about dying; it is about any stretch where you cannot
      see the way out.
  - term: Rod and staff
    plain: >-
      Two tools of the trade. The rod was a club for driving off predators; the
      staff was the hooked stick for guiding and catching hold of sheep. One
      protects, one directs, and David finds both of them reassuring.
  - term: Anoint my head with oil
    plain: >-
      Pouring perfumed oil on a guest's head was ordinary hospitality at a good
      meal, like taking someone's coat and pouring them a drink. It signalled
      that the guest was welcome and valued.
  - term: Mercy
    plain: >-
      The Hebrew word is hesed — loyal, committed kindness that keeps its
      promises. It is not pity. It is the faithfulness of someone who has decided
      to stay on your side.
keyVerses:
  - usfm: PSA.23.1
    why: >-
      The line most people know even if they know nothing else in the Bible.
      Everything else in the psalm unpacks it. Read as a whole, it is a claim
      that the God who runs the universe takes personal, practical
      responsibility for one person's needs.
  - usfm: PSA.23.4
    why: >-
      The verse read at more funerals and hospital bedsides than any other in
      the Bible, and the reason this psalm endures. Its comfort is precisely
      located: not that the valley is removed, but that you are not walking it
      by yourself.
  - usfm: PSA.23.6
    why: >-
      The closing promise, and stronger in Hebrew than in most English versions —
      God's goodness and loyal love actively chase David down for the rest of his
      life. It answers the fear that God's kindness might run out before you do.
caution: >-
  Psalm 23 is often quoted as though it promises a comfortable life, and it does
  not. There is a dark valley in verse 4 and there are enemies in verse 5, and
  the psalm never says either one gets removed. "I shall not want" is a promise
  about lacking nothing you need, not about getting everything you ask for —
  reading it the other way sets people up to feel that God broke his word when
  something goes wrong. It is also worth naming that David wrote this as a man
  who spent years being hunted, not as someone whose life had gone smoothly. The
  psalm is a statement of trust made from inside trouble. Quoted to someone in
  pain as a reason they should not feel bad, it becomes the opposite of what it
  is.
faqs:
  - q: Who wrote Psalm 23 and when?
    a: >-
      The psalm is headed "A psalm of David", and David spent his early life as
      a working shepherd before becoming king of Israel around 1000 BC. The
      shepherding detail in it — still water rather than moving water, the rod
      and the staff as two different tools — reads like someone describing a job
      they had actually done rather than a metaphor they had read about.
  - q: What does "I shall not want" actually mean?
    a: >-
      It means "I will not lack anything I need". "Want" is being used in the
      older English sense of being short of something, the way we still say a
      house is "wanting a roof". David is not claiming he will always get what he
      desires. He is claiming that with this shepherd, nothing genuinely
      necessary will be missing.
  - q: What is the valley of the shadow of death?
    a: >-
      Literally a deep, dark ravine — the most dangerous stretch of ground a
      flock could be moved through, where predators had cover and the sheep could
      not see far. Many translations render it "the darkest valley". It certainly
      includes dying, which is why the verse is read at funerals, but it applies
      to any period you cannot see your way out of.
  - q: Why does the psalm switch from sheep to a dinner table?
    a: >-
      Because it is making the same point twice with two different pictures. In
      the first half God is the shepherd out in the open who keeps David alive.
      In the second he is the host indoors who treats David as an honoured guest.
      Provision and welcome. The shift also moves the psalm from surviving to
      being celebrated, which is a deliberate escalation.
  - q: Is Psalm 23 about Jesus?
    a: >-
      Christians read it that way, and with reason: in John 10 Jesus calls
      himself the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep, which is a
      direct echo. Hebrews 13:20 and 1 Peter 2:25 use the same image of him. The
      psalm was written centuries earlier and stands on its own, but the New
      Testament clearly picks up the picture and applies it to Jesus.
  - q: Why is Psalm 23 read at funerals?
    a: >-
      Mostly because of verse 4. It is one of the few well-known passages that
      names death directly without flinching and without pretending the loss is
      small, while still offering something — company rather than an
      explanation. Verse 6's promise of dwelling in God's house for ever gives
      the psalm somewhere to end that a funeral needs.

## If this is your first time reading Psalm 23

It is six verses long. Read the whole thing before reading anything about it —
that takes under a minute and it is the only way to notice the turn in the
middle, where David stops describing God and starts speaking to him. Verses 1 to
3 say *he*. Verse 4 says *you*. Something happens in the valley that changes how
David talks.

The psalm is also worth reading slowly enough to see what it does not promise.
It is not a guarantee of an easy life from someone who had one. It is a statement
of confidence from a man with enemies, written in the language of a job he used
to do.
