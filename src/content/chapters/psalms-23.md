usfm: PSA.23
title: "Psalm 23 Explained: The Lord Is My Shepherd"
shortAnswer: >-
  In Psalm 23, David describes God as a shepherd who provides, guides, and stays
  close through danger. The six verses use two pictures. God first leads a flock
  through open country, then welcomes a guest to a full table. A dark valley and
  hostile people remain in view, but David's confidence rests on God's faithful
  presence through them.
outline:
  - verses: "1"
    heading: The opening claim carries the whole psalm
    note: >-
      "The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want." Everything else in the psalm
      shows what that sentence means in practice. The word "my" makes David's
      statement personal. He is describing a relationship he knows.
  - verses: "2-3"
    heading: The shepherd provides and restores
    note: >-
      Green grass, quiet water, and renewed life fill these verses. The phrase "still
      waters" fits a shepherd's care because sheep need a safe place to drink.
      God provides in a form the creature can receive.
  - verses: "4"
    heading: God's presence changes the darkest valley
    note: >-
      As the path enters its darkest place, David stops talking about God and
      begins speaking directly to him. The valley remains, but the shepherd is
      close enough to address.
  - verses: "5"
    heading: The shepherd becomes a generous host
    note: >-
      The scene shifts to a prepared table, fragrant oil, and an overflowing
      cup. David's enemies are still present, yet God publicly welcomes and
      honors him as a valued guest.
  - verses: "6"
    heading: Goodness and mercy follow David home
    note: >-
      The verb translated "follow" can also mean "pursue." Goodness and loyal love
      accompany the speaker throughout life, and the closing line looks toward a
      lasting place in the Lord's house.
themes:
  - name: God as a present shepherd
    explanation: >-
      Shepherding in ancient Israel was unglamorous, physical, round-the-clock
      work. A shepherd walked with the flock, slept near it, and personally
      faced its dangers. Calling God a shepherd presents him as close, attentive,
      and actively involved in the flock's care.
  - name: Presence through danger
    explanation: >-
      The most quoted line in the psalm is about walking through the valley of
      the shadow of death. David still walks through the valley, saying, "I will
      fear no evil, for you are with me". God's presence is the center of the
      promise, not an easy route around every danger.
  - name: Enough for the journey
    explanation: >-
      "I shall not want" means "I will not lack". A well-shepherded sheep still
      faces weather, distance, and predators. The claim concerns faithful
      provision for real needs, not the fulfillment of every desire.
  - name: Public honor in front of enemies
    explanation: >-
      Verse 5 seats David at a full table while his enemies watch. In a culture
      shaped by public honor and shame, God's welcome provides visible
      vindication before people who wanted David humiliated.
glossary:
  - term: The Lord
    plain: >-
      Where your Bible prints LORD in capitals, the Hebrew has God's personal
      name, revealed to Moses. David's first line names the particular God he
      knows as his shepherd.
  - term: I shall not want
    plain: >-
      Older English for "I will not lack anything I need". Here "want" means to
      be short of something. David is speaking about provision, not every wish.
  - term: Restores my soul
    plain: >-
      Brings me back to life. The Hebrew word for soul means the whole living
      person. The phrase is close to "he revives me" and includes the renewal of
      the whole self.
  - term: The valley of the shadow of death
    plain: >-
      An image of a valley in deep darkness. Some translations use "darkest
      valley"; others read "shadow of death." It can speak to the fear of death
      or other dangers where the way forward is hard to see.
  - term: Rod and staff
    plain: >-
      A shepherd's rod and staff are tools associated with protecting and guiding
      a flock. The psalm does not explain the separate use of each tool; together
      they reassure the speaker that the shepherd is present.
  - term: Anoint my head with oil
    plain: >-
      Pouring perfumed oil on a guest's head was ordinary hospitality at a good
      meal, something like taking a guest's coat and offering a drink. It signaled
      that the guest was welcome and valued.
  - term: Mercy
    plain: >-
      The Hebrew word *hesed* describes loyal, committed kindness that keeps its
      promises. It is the faithful love of someone who has chosen to stay.
keyVerses:
  - usfm: PSA.23.1
    why: >-
      This is the psalm's best-known line, and every later verse develops it.
      David trusts the God who rules the world to take personal, practical care
      of his needs.
  - usfm: PSA.23.4
    why: >-
      Often read at funerals and hospital bedsides, this verse locates comfort
      in God's company through the valley. The walker isn't alone.
  - usfm: PSA.23.6
    why: >-
      The Hebrew verb makes the closing promise especially active. God's
      goodness and loyal love pursue David throughout his life, answering the
      fear that divine kindness might run out.
caution: >-
  Psalm 23 doesn't promise a comfortable life. Verse 4 enters a dark valley, and
  verse 5 keeps the enemies in view. "I shall not want" speaks of receiving what
  is needed; it doesn't guarantee every desired outcome. David knew years of
  danger and pursuit, so his trust comes from inside a troubled life. Using the
  psalm to tell someone in pain that they shouldn't feel bad works against the
  comfort it offers.
faqs:
  - q: Who wrote Psalm 23 and when?
    a: >-
      The psalm's heading associates it with David, whom the Bible describes as
      a shepherd before he became king. The heading does not say when the psalm
      was written, and scholars differ over how such headings relate to authorship.
      Its exact date and circumstances cannot be established from the poem.
  - q: What does "I shall not want" mean?
    a: >-
      It means "I will not lack anything I need". "Want" is being used in the
      older English sense of being short of something, the way we still say a
      house is "wanting a roof". David is claiming faithful provision from his
      shepherd, not the fulfillment of every desire.
  - q: What is the valley of the shadow of death?
    a: >-
      The Hebrew can be translated "deep darkness," so many Bibles read "the
      darkest valley." Older translations use "the valley of the shadow of death."
      The image can comfort someone facing death or another grave danger. The
      reason given for courage is that the shepherd is present in the valley.
  - q: Why does the psalm switch from sheep to a dinner table?
    a: >-
      Many readers hear a shift from shepherd to host in verse 5: God prepares
      a meal, anoints the guest with oil, and fills the cup while enemies watch.
      Others understand the shepherd image to continue. Either way, the table
      presents generous welcome with danger still in view.
  - q: What does "my cup overflows" mean in Psalm 23?
    a: >-
      The full cup is part of the meal God prepares in Psalm 23:5. Together with
      the oil and the table, it pictures a host's generous welcome even while
      enemies are present. It expresses abundance within the poem, not a guarantee
      that every believer will have material wealth.
  - q: What does dwelling in the house of the Lord forever mean?
    a: >-
      Psalm 23:6 ends with the speaker's hope of remaining in God's presence.
      The Hebrew phrase can mean "for length of days," and some translations
      emphasize a long life. Many Christians also hear hope of life with God
      beyond death. The verse follows a promise of goodness throughout life.
  - q: Is Psalm 23 about Jesus?
    a: >-
      Christians read it that way, and with reason: in John 10 Jesus calls
      himself the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep. Hebrews
      13:20 and 1 Peter 2:25 also use shepherd imagery for him. The
      psalm was written centuries earlier and has its own original setting. The
      New Testament later takes up its shepherd image and applies it to Jesus.
  - q: Why is Psalm 23 read at funerals?
    a: >-
      Its picture of God accompanying someone through deep darkness gives words
      of comfort at a death or funeral. The final hope of dwelling in the Lord's
      house has also been read as hope beyond death. The Hebrew can be understood
      as a long life in God's presence, so the psalm need not be limited to funerals.

## If this is your first time reading Psalm 23

Psalm 23 is only six verses long, and reading it straight through takes less
than a minute. The turn in the middle is easy to miss. In verses 1 through 3,
David says *he* while describing God. In verse 4, inside the valley, he begins
saying *you*. Danger draws the prayer into direct address.

The psalm deserves a slow reading because its confidence doesn't come from an
easy life. David speaks as a man with enemies and uses the language of work he
once did. His hope is grounded in the shepherd's presence through the whole
route.
