shortAnswer: >-
  Psalm 46 presents God as a secure refuge while the earth shakes and nations rage.
  Its famous command, "Be still, and know that I am God," follows a scene where God
  ends wars and destroys weapons. In context, the words call the nations to stop and
  recognize God's rule. The poem contrasts a roaring sea with a life-giving river,
  then repeats its central assurance twice: the LORD of Hosts is with us.

themes:
  - name: "Be still calls the nations to lay down their fight"
    explanation: >-
      Verse 10 comes directly after God breaks bows, shatters spears, and burns
      shields. The Hebrew verb translated "be still" can mean to let go, slacken, or
      cease, which is why some translations say "cease striving." The next line looks
      toward God's exaltation among the nations. In this setting, the command stops
      human conflict and asserts God's authority. Quiet prayer may echo its trust, but
      warfare supplies the verse's immediate setting.
  - name: Chaotic water and a steady river tell two stories
    explanation: >-
      Water carries two different meanings across the poem. The sea roars and swallows
      mountains in verses 2 and 3, an ancient picture of chaos. Verse 4 introduces a
      river whose streams refresh God's city. The city is still surrounded by a
      troubled world, yet it receives a steady source of life within. The contrast
      makes God's sustaining presence visible.
  - name: 'The refrain is "God with us"'
    explanation: >-
      Verses 7 and 11 repeat the same line, framing the final part of the poem. The
      Hebrew word translated "with us" is immanu, which also appears in the name
      Immanuel used in Isaiah and later applied to Jesus in Matthew. Psalm 46 answers
      upheaval with the assurance of God's presence. The repeated refrain gives that
      assurance the final word.
  - name: God's presence provides stability within trouble
    explanation: >-
      Verse 5 says the city will not be moved because God is within it, even as nations
      rage nearby. Verse 1 similarly locates God's help in the middle of trouble. The
      psalm offers steadiness and refuge while danger remains present. That grounded
      promise has allowed frightened communities to pray these words for centuries.

caution: >-
  "Be still, and know that I am God" is often used to encourage quiet reflection.
  That application can express trust, but the verse first addresses a world at war.
  The preceding lines describe destroyed weapons, and the rest of verse 10 announces
  God's exaltation among the nations. The command calls people to cease their struggle
  and acknowledge God's rule. The opening verses also deserve careful use. They do
  not promise that disaster will never happen. They describe confidence in God even
  if mountains fall into the sea. Psalm 46 offers God's presence and protection amid
  upheaval, without guaranteeing freedom from crisis or violence.
faqs:
  - q: What does "be still and know that I am God" mean?
    a: >-
      The Hebrew verb can mean to let go, slacken, or cease. The previous verses show
      God breaking bows, shattering spears, and burning shields, so the command tells
      warring nations to stop. The rest of the verse announces that God will be exalted
      among the nations and throughout the earth. Some translations capture this sense
      with "cease striving." The words call people to release their fight and recognize
      God's authority.
  - q: Is "be still and know that I am God" about quiet time or about war?
    a: >-
      War provides the immediate setting. Verses 8 and 9 describe God ending conflicts
      and destroying weapons, and verse 10 speaks of his rule among the nations. The
      psalm does not describe solitude or a private devotional practice. Christians
      have still drawn personal comfort from it for centuries, since God's presence in
      a collapsing world speaks naturally to anxiety. Quiet reflection is a reasonable
      response, as long as the verse's wider call for nations to stop fighting remains
      in view.
  - q: What does "God is our refuge and strength, a very present help" mean?
    a: >-
      Refuge describes a safe place, strength supplies what frightened people lack,
      and "very present" conveys help that is readily found. The final words, "in
      trouble," place God's care inside the crisis. The collapsing mountains and
      raging nations that follow develop the same idea. God's people can find him
      available and dependable even when their surroundings are unstable.
  - q: What is the river whose streams make glad the city of God?
    a: >-
      Jerusalem has springs and cisterns but no large river, which makes this a poetic
      image. Its calm streams answer the roaring sea in verses 2 and 3. Readers have
      also connected the river with Eden and the river in Revelation 22. Across those
      passages, flowing water pictures the life and provision that come from God's
      presence.
  - q: What does Selah mean?
    a: >-
      Its exact meaning is unknown. Selah appears more than seventy times in Psalms,
      including three times here, and it was probably a musical direction. Suggestions
      include a pause, an instrumental interlude, a change in volume, or a cue to
      "lift up" voices or instruments. In Psalm 46 it marks the end of each major section, so a
      deliberate pause fits its placement even though certainty is out of reach.
  - q: Why is "the LORD of Hosts is with us" repeated?
    a: >-
      Repetition gives the line special weight and frames the second half of the poem.
      The Hebrew word for "with us," immanu, also appears in the name Immanuel used by
      Isaiah and later applied to Jesus in Matthew. By stating the refrain in verses 7
      and 11, the singers answer earthquakes, floods, and war with the same assurance:
      God is present with his people.
  - q: Did Martin Luther write "A Mighty Fortress" from this psalm?
    a: >-
      Yes. Martin Luther wrote the German hymn "Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott", known
      in English as "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God", in the late 1520s. It paraphrases
      Psalm 46 and became the best-known hymn of the Reformation. The title comes from
      the fortress language repeated in verses 7 and 11. Reports that Luther turned to
      this psalm during alarming times fit its message of trust amid public upheaval.

## If this is your first time reading Psalm 46

Psalm 46 is only eleven verses long. Its best-known line comes near the end, and
verses 8 and 9 establish the scene you need for understanding it.

The opening imagines creation coming apart. Earth shifts, mountains fall into the
sea, and the water roars. The singers still call God their refuge and say they will
not fear. The next scene moves inside a city where a river brings gladness. While
chaotic water surges outside, this steady stream pictures God's sustaining presence.

Verses 8 and 9 invite readers to see God's works. He ends wars by breaking bows,
shattering spears, and burning shields. The devastation falls on the weapons that
have devastated people.

God then says, "Be still, and know that I am God." The Hebrew verb can mean to let
go or cease, and the rest of the verse speaks to the nations. In its first setting,
this is a command to stop fighting and recognize the authority of the God who has
disarmed the battlefield.

The verse can still comfort someone in a quiet room. Its comfort grows stronger when
the larger setting remains visible: God is able to halt violence that no human power
can control.

Verses 7 and 11 repeat the same refrain: the LORD of Hosts is with us, and the God of
Jacob is our fortress. The Hebrew word immanu means "with us" and also appears in the
name Immanuel. After earthquakes, floods, and war, Psalm 46 closes with the presence
of God as its deepest source of security.
