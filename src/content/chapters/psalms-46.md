usfm: PSA.46
title: "Psalm 46: Be Still Is Said to Armies, Not to Quiet Rooms"
shortAnswer: >-
  "Be still, and know that I am God" is printed on candles and devotional apps as an
  invitation to sit quietly, and in its own psalm it is spoken on a battlefield. The
  two verses before it have God breaking bows, shattering spears and burning shields,
  and the Hebrew verb behind "be still" means to let go, drop, or stop — closer to
  "put your weapons down" than to "calm your mind". The psalm is built on a contrast
  between two kinds of water: a roaring sea swallowing mountains outside, and a
  quiet river running through the city. And its refrain, said twice, is the phrase
  behind the name Immanuel: God with us.
outline:
  - verses: "1-3"
    heading: The worst thing imaginable, and a refusal to be afraid of it
    note: >-
      God as refuge, strength and a help already present in trouble — trouble is
      assumed, not denied. Then the scale: the earth changing, mountains toppling into
      the sea, the water roaring and foaming. If that happened, the psalm says, we
      would still not fear.
  - verses: "4-7"
    heading: A city with a river running through it
    note: >-
      Against the roaring sea, a river whose streams bring gladness. God is inside
      the city, so it will not be moved — note that it is not said to be
      unthreatened. Nations rage and kingdoms crumble outside. Then the refrain: the
      LORD of Hosts is with us.
  - verses: "8-9"
    heading: Come and look at the wreckage
    note: >-
      An invitation to inspect what God has done, and what is lying broken turns out
      to be weapons. He makes wars stop, breaks the bow, snaps the spear, burns the
      shields. The devastation in verse 8 is devastation done to the means of
      devastation.
  - verses: "10-11"
    heading: God speaks once, and the refrain closes it
    note: >-
      The only words God says in the psalm: be still and know that I am God, I will
      be exalted among the nations and over the earth. Then the refrain again, word
      for word, as the last line.
themes:
  - name: 'Be still is addressed to combatants'
    explanation: >-
      Verse 10 is God's only speech in the psalm, and it arrives immediately after two
      verses about disarmament — bows broken, spears shattered, shields burned. The
      Hebrew verb translated "be still" means to let go, slacken, drop or cease; some
      translations render it "cease striving" or simply "stop". Put back into its
      paragraph, it is what God says to raging nations once their weapons are in
      pieces, and the second half of the verse confirms the audience: I will be
      exalted among the nations. None of that makes quiet prayer a bad idea. It does
      mean the verse is not about it.
  - name: Two kinds of water
    explanation: >-
      The psalm's images are organised around water and it is worth noticing which is
      which. Outside, in verses 2 and 3, the sea roars and foams and mountains fall
      into it — the ancient picture of chaos at its most complete. Inside, in verse 4,
      there is a river, and rivers are steady, fresh and useful. The city of God is
      not described as dry or as walled off from weather; it has water running through
      it while water rages outside. That is the psalm's whole claim in one image:
      the difference is not the absence of chaos but the presence of something else.
  - name: The refrain is "God with us"
    explanation: >-
      Verses 7 and 11 are identical, which in Hebrew poetry is a way of putting a
      frame around what sits between them. The phrase they both turn on — the LORD of
      Hosts is with us — uses the word immanu, which is the second half of the name
      Immanuel that Isaiah later gives and Matthew applies to Jesus. So the psalm's
      answer to a collapsing world is not an explanation, a strategy or a promise of
      calm weather. It is a preposition. Twice.
  - name: The city is not spared trouble, it is not moved
    explanation: >-
      Read verse 5 carefully: God is within her, she will not be moved. It does not say
      she will not be attacked, and verse 6 has nations raging in the immediate
      vicinity. The same is true of verse 1, which calls God a help in trouble rather
      than a way around it. The psalm is precise about what it offers, and that
      precision is why it has been sung by people in genuinely bad situations for
      three thousand years. It promises footing, not weather.
glossary:
  - term: Selah
    plain: >-
      A word appearing three times in this psalm and often in the Psalms, and nobody
      is certain what it means. The best guesses are a musical direction — a pause, a
      louder passage, an instrumental break. Here it falls after each of the psalm's
      three sections, which fits the idea of a break.
  - term: refuge
    plain: >-
      A place you go to be safe. The Hebrew word suggests shelter and concealment, and
      the psalm pairs it immediately with strength, so it is not only somewhere to
      hide but something that holds.
  - term: the LORD of Hosts
    plain: >-
      A title meaning roughly the commander of armies. In a psalm about nations at war
      and weapons being destroyed, the choice of title is pointed: the one who ends
      the fighting is described in military terms.
  - term: the God of Jacob
    plain: >-
      God identified by his relationship with a particular ancestor rather than by his
      power. Jacob was a deceiver who was renamed Israel, so the title carries a note
      of God sticking with people who did not deserve it.
  - term: be still
    plain: >-
      The Hebrew verb means to let go, drop, slacken or leave off. It is the word for
      relaxing your grip on something. That is why "cease striving" and "stop" are both
      defensible translations, and why "quieten your heart" is a stretch.
  - term: the city of God
    plain: >-
      Jerusalem, where the temple stood and where God's presence was understood to
      dwell. Verse 4's river is not a literal one — Jerusalem has no river — which is
      part of why the image reads as a picture of provision rather than geography.

caution: >-
  "Be still, and know that I am God" has become a devotional slogan about quietness,
  and the psalm will not quite support the weight. The verb means to let go or drop
  something, the two preceding verses describe God destroying weapons, and the rest
  of the sentence says "I will be exalted among the nations" — an audience of
  countries, not of individuals in a quiet room. Read in place it is closer to a
  command to stop fighting and an announcement of who is actually in charge. That is
  not a reason to stop praying quietly, which the Bible commends in plenty of other
  places. It is a reason not to build a practice on a verse that is doing something
  else, because the verse's real force is considerably stronger than the poster
  version: the God telling everybody to stand down has just broken every weapon on
  the field. The other thing worth reading carefully is verses 2 and 3. They do not
  promise that mountains will stay put. They say that if they fell into the sea, the
  psalm's singers would still not be afraid.
faqs:
  - q: What does "be still and know that I am God" mean?
    a: >-
      The Hebrew verb means to let go, drop, slacken or cease — it is the word for
      relaxing your grip on something you are holding. Read with the two verses before
      it, where God breaks bows, shatters spears and burns shields, it is what God says
      to nations at war once their weapons are destroyed: stop. The second half of the
      verse names the audience and the reason — I will be exalted among the nations, I
      will be exalted over the earth. So "know that I am God" is not an invitation to
      reflect; it is closer to a statement of who has just settled the matter. Some
      translations render the first phrase "cease striving", which is nearer the sense.
  - q: Is "be still and know that I am God" about quiet time or about war?
    a: >-
      About war, on the plain reading of its own context. Verses 8 and 9 invite the
      hearer to come and look at what God has done, and what God has done is end
      fighting and destroy the equipment for it. Verse 10 follows directly, and its own
      second half addresses nations. Nothing in the psalm mentions solitude, silence
      or personal devotion. That said, the verse has been used devotionally for
      centuries and the instinct behind that is not baseless — a psalm which says God
      is present while the world falls apart obviously has something to say to an
      anxious person. The honest position is that the comfort is real and the specific
      words are about something bigger than a morning routine.
  - q: What does "God is our refuge and strength, a very present help" mean?
    a: >-
      Three claims in one line. Refuge is somewhere to shelter; strength is what you
      draw on when your own runs out; and the third phrase, often translated "ever-present"
      or "very present", means found right there, immediately available. The words that
      finish the sentence matter most: help in trouble. The psalm does not open by
      promising an absence of trouble, it opens by assuming trouble and locating the
      help inside it. Everything that follows — the collapsing mountains, the raging
      nations — is consistent with that. The offer is not evacuation.
  - q: What is the river whose streams make glad the city of God?
    a: >-
      Not a literal river, since Jerusalem does not have one — it is supplied by
      springs and cisterns, which is part of the point. The image works by contrast
      with verses 2 and 3, where the sea roars and foams and mountains fall into it.
      Outside there is chaotic water; inside there is a steady stream that gladdens.
      Readers have connected it with the river in Eden and with the river in the last
      chapter of Revelation, both of which do similar work: a picture of continuous,
      calm provision at the centre of a place where God is.
  - q: What does Selah mean?
    a: >-
      Nobody knows for certain. It occurs seventy-odd times in the Psalms, three of
      them in this one, and the most widely accepted guesses are musical: a pause, a
      change in volume, or an instrumental interlude. Some have suggested it means
      "lift up", either voices or instruments. In Psalm 46 it falls at the end of each
      of the three sections — after the collapsing mountains, after the first refrain,
      and at the very end — which fits the idea of a break, and reading it as a
      deliberate pause is a reasonable way to use it even without certainty.
  - q: Why is "the LORD of Hosts is with us" repeated?
    a: >-
      Because repetition is how Hebrew poetry emphasises and frames. Verses 7 and 11
      are word for word identical, which puts a border around the middle of the psalm
      and makes the phrase its conclusion twice over. The important word is "with us",
      immanu in Hebrew — the same word that begins the name Immanuel, which Isaiah
      later uses and Matthew applies to Jesus. So the psalm's answer to earthquakes,
      floods and raging nations is not a plan or an explanation. It is the claim that
      God is present, stated once in the middle and once at the end.
  - q: Did Martin Luther write "A Mighty Fortress" from this psalm?
    a: >-
      Yes. "Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott" — usually sung in English as "A Mighty
      Fortress Is Our God" — is Luther's paraphrase of Psalm 46, written in the late
      1520s, and it became the best-known hymn of the Reformation. The psalm's fortress
      language in verses 7 and 11 is the direct source of the title. Luther is also
      reported to have turned to this psalm when the news was bad, telling companions
      to come and sing the forty-sixth. Given what the psalm actually says about
      collapsing mountains and raging nations, that is a fair use of it.

## If this is your first time reading Psalm 46

Eleven verses, and the famous one is second to last. Read verses 8 and 9 before you
read verse 10, because they change it completely.

Here is what happens. The psalm opens with the worst thing its writers could
picture: the earth changing shape, mountains toppling into the sea, the water roaring
and foaming. And it says, if that happened, we would not be afraid. Then it moves
inside a city with a river running through it — quiet water against the chaos
outside — and says God is in there, so the city will not be moved.

Then verse 8: come and see what the LORD has done. And what he has done, in verse 9,
is stop wars. Break the bow. Snap the spear. Burn the shields.

Now read verse 10. "Be still, and know that I am God." It is not spoken into a quiet
room. It is spoken over a field of broken weapons, to the nations that were using
them, and the Hebrew verb means to let go, to drop something, to stop. The rest of
the sentence says who is listening: I will be exalted among the nations, I will be
exalted over the earth.

That is a much bigger sentence than the one on the candle. It is not an invitation to
relax. It is an order to stand down, issued by someone who has just disarmed
everybody.

One last thing. Verses 7 and 11 are the same words twice: the LORD of Hosts is with
us, the God of Jacob is our fortress. The phrase "with us" is immanu, which you have
met before — it is the front half of Immanuel. After all the earthquakes and the
armies, the psalm's answer is not a strategy or a promise about the weather. It is
that word, repeated, at the end of each half.
