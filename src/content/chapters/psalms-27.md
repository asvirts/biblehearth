usfm: PSA.27
title: "Psalm 27: It Says Whom Shall I Fear, Then Begs God Not to Leave"
seoTitle: "Psalm 27: Whom Shall I Fear, Then a Plea"
shortAnswer: >-
  Psalm 27 opens with the most confident line David ever wrote — the LORD is my
  light and my salvation, whom shall I fear — and six verses later the same voice
  is pleading: do not hide your face from me, do not leave me or forsake me. The
  psalm never explains the change and never apologises for it. Two other things
  make it unusual. The one thing he says he wants is not rescue from the army
  camped around him in verse 3; it is to live in God's house and look at him. And
  it ends not with the danger gone but with an instruction, given twice, to wait.
outline:
  - verses: "1-3"
    heading: Confidence put as two questions nobody is meant to answer
    note: >-
      Light, salvation, stronghold — three words for God, then "whom shall I
      fear?" and "whom shall I dread?" Verse 2 remembers an attack that already
      failed, and verse 3 raises the stakes: a whole army encamped, and a heart
      that says it will not be afraid.
  - verses: "4"
    heading: One request, and it is not the obvious one
    note: >-
      A man describing an army around him says there is one thing he has asked
      for — and it is to live in the LORD's house all his life, gazing at his
      beauty. Not deliverance, not victory, not safety. Access.
  - verses: "5-6"
    heading: What he expects that access to be worth
    note: >-
      Hidden in God's shelter, concealed under his tent, set high on a rock, head
      lifted above the enemies still standing around him. Then music: sacrifices
      with shouting, singing, playing. The confidence section ends in a party.
  - verses: "7-9"
    heading: The voice changes completely
    note: >-
      "Hear, O LORD, my voice when I call; be merciful and answer me." Then a
      quiet exchange — his own heart tells him to seek God's face, and he agrees
      to. And then the pleading: do not hide your face, do not turn me away, do
      not leave me or forsake me.
  - verses: "10-12"
    heading: The fears finally get named
    note: >-
      Being abandoned even by his parents. Not knowing which way to go. Being
      handed over to people who want him dead, and false witnesses who are
      "breathing out violence". These are specific, and they are not the army of
      verse 3.
  - verses: "13-14"
    heading: A decision, and an instruction repeated
    note: >-
      "Still I am certain to see the goodness of the LORD in the land of the
      living." Not "I have seen" — he is deciding to expect it. Then the last
      verse says wait for the LORD, be strong and take heart, wait for the LORD.
      Nothing has been resolved.
themes:
  - name: Confidence and pleading in the same voice
    explanation: >-
      Read verses 1 to 6 and then verses 7 to 12 back to back and they sound like
      two different people. Some readers conclude the psalm is two poems joined
      together, and it may be. But whoever put them in this order was making a
      point that a tidier psalm could not make. "Whom shall I fear" is not a mood
      this writer sustains for fourteen verses, and "do not forsake me" is not a
      failure of the faith in verse 1. They are both in here, minutes apart, with
      no transition and no embarrassment. For a reader who swings between the two
      in a single afternoon, that is the most useful thing about the psalm.
  - name: The one thing he asks for is not safety
    explanation: >-
      Verse 4 is the psalm's centre and its strangest line. Having described an
      army encamped around him, David says there is one thing he has asked of the
      LORD and one thing he desires — and it turns out to be dwelling in God's
      house, looking at his beauty, seeking him in his temple. He is not asking to
      be got out. He is asking to be let in. Whatever the psalm is confident
      about, its confidence does not rest on the outcome of the siege, which is why
      it can survive not knowing how the siege ends.
  - name: The worst fear named is being left
    explanation: >-
      Count what he actually asks not to happen. Do not hide your face. Do not turn
      your servant away. Do not leave me or forsake me. Though my father and mother
      forsake me. Four times in five verses, the danger is abandonment rather than
      death — and verse 10 reaches for the two people who are supposed to be the
      last to go. The army in verse 3 gets one line. Being left alone gets the
      whole middle of the psalm, which tells you what this writer was most afraid
      of.
  - name: It ends in waiting, not in rescue
    explanation: >-
      Nothing is resolved by verse 14. The enemies are still there, the false
      witnesses have not been answered, and God has not visibly done anything. What
      the psalm offers instead is a decision — I am certain I will see God's
      goodness — followed by an instruction it repeats for emphasis: wait for the
      LORD. That is a deliberately unsatisfying ending, and it is honest. Most
      people praying this psalm are not on the far side of their trouble. They are
      in the middle of it, being told to hold on and that holding on is not
      nothing.
glossary:
  - term: stronghold
    plain: >-
      A fortified place built to survive an attack, not just to hide in. Verse 1
      calls God the stronghold of David's life, which is a stronger image than
      shelter — it assumes the attack comes and holds anyway.
  - term: the beauty of the LORD
    plain: >-
      What David says he wants to gaze at in verse 4. The Hebrew word covers
      pleasantness and delightfulness as much as appearance, so it is closer to
      wanting to be near someone you find wonderful than to admiring a view.
  - term: tabernacle
    plain: >-
      The tent that served as Israel's place of worship before the temple was
      built — portable, and the place God's presence was understood to be. When the
      psalm talks about God's house or tent, this is what it means.
  - term: seek his face
    plain: >-
      An old way of saying seek someone's favour and attention, the way you would
      want a king to look at you rather than past you. Verse 8 has David's own
      heart telling him to do it, and him agreeing.
  - term: the land of the living
    plain: >-
      Simply this life, among people who are still alive, as opposed to death.
      Verse 13's hope is deliberately down-to-earth: David expects to see God do
      something good before he dies, not only afterwards.
  - term: false witnesses
    plain: >-
      People giving untrue evidence against someone in a public hearing. In a
      world where cases were decided at the town gate by testimony, a false witness
      could get you killed, which is why verse 12 puts them beside people breathing
      out violence.

caution: >-
  Verse 1 gets quoted on its own as though it settled the question of fear for
  anybody who believes properly — the LORD is my light and my salvation, so a
  Christian who is frightened must be doing something wrong. The psalm itself is
  the answer to that. The same writer who asks "whom shall I fear" spends verses 7
  to 12 asking God not to leave him, naming abandonment by his own parents, and
  asking not to be handed over to people who want him dead. Nobody edited that out.
  Whoever arranged this psalm was content to put unshakeable confidence and open
  pleading in the same fourteen verses without reconciling them, and a reader who
  only ever hears verse 1 will conclude their own experience of praying is a
  failure when it is in fact what the rest of the psalm sounds like. The other thing
  worth watching is verse 14. "Wait for the LORD" is not advice to be passive or to
  do nothing about a solvable problem; the same verse tells you to be strong and
  take heart, which is effort. It is about not forcing an outcome you cannot force,
  not about declining to act.
faqs:
  - q: What does "the LORD is my light and my salvation" mean?
    a: >-
      Light in the Psalms usually stands for the ability to see where you are going
      and for relief after a bad night — so calling God his light is closer to "he
      is how I can see" than to a statement about brightness. Salvation here means
      rescue in a concrete sense: being got out of danger. Put together with the
      third title in the same verse, the stronghold of my life, David is naming
      three things a person in trouble needs — orientation, rescue, and somewhere
      that holds — and saying all three are the same person. The two questions that
      follow are not claims that he never feels afraid. They are what he is arguing
      himself into.
  - q: What is the one thing David asks for in Psalm 27:4?
    a: >-
      To live in the LORD's house all the days of his life, to gaze on his beauty
      and to seek him in his temple. What makes it remarkable is the verse before
      it: an army is camped around him. Given one request, he does not ask to be
      rescued, to win, or to be safe — he asks for permanent access to God's
      presence. Practically, for David that would have meant the tent where Israel
      worshipped rather than a building. The point of the verse is the ranking. The
      thing he wants most is not an outcome; it is nearness, and that is why the
      psalm can end without the danger being resolved.
  - q: Why does Psalm 27 change from confidence to pleading halfway through?
    a: >-
      Nobody knows, and it is one of the most discussed features of the psalm. Some
      scholars think two originally separate poems were joined, since verses 1 to 6
      are a psalm of confidence and verses 7 to 14 read as a lament. Others read one
      person's prayer moving from what he knows to what he feels. Either way, the
      arrangement is the useful part. Whoever finalised this psalm was willing to
      leave both halves in, without a transition and without treating the second
      half as a lapse. Confidence and desperate asking sit side by side as the same
      faith, which is how praying actually goes for most people.
  - q: What does "though my father and mother forsake me" mean?
    a: >-
      It is the psalm reaching for the worst case it can imagine. In that world your
      family was your security — your food, your legal standing, your protection —
      and your parents were the people who by definition did not abandon you. Verse
      10 supposes even that gone, and answers it in half a line: the LORD will
      receive me. The Hebrew word is warm, closer to gathering somebody in than to
      granting them an audience. Whether David's own parents actually failed him is
      not stated and probably not the point. He is saying that the floor beneath
      him is lower than the lowest thing that can be taken away.
  - q: What does "wait for the LORD, be strong and take heart" mean?
    a: >-
      The waiting here is not passive and not resigned. The same verse pairs it with
      being strong and taking courage, and the psalm repeats the command at the
      start and end of the sentence, which in Hebrew is a way of underlining it. The
      situation being waited on is unresolved — enemies still present, accusations
      unanswered — so what is being asked for is the strength to keep expecting God
      to act without being able to make him act on your timetable. It is the
      hardest instruction in the psalm and the last word it leaves you with.
  - q: What does "the land of the living" mean in Psalm 27:13?
    a: >-
      It means this life, here, among people who are still alive. The phrase turns
      up elsewhere in the Old Testament as a plain contrast with being dead, and
      David is using it to locate his hope in a specific place. He is not saying he
      expects things to be put right after he dies; he is saying he expects to see
      God do something good while he is still around to see it. That makes verse 13
      a riskier statement than a general hope about eternity, which is probably why
      the very next verse tells him to wait.
  - q: What does "seek his face" mean?
    a: >-
      Seeking someone's face is an old way of describing wanting their attention and
      favour — the difference between a king looking at you and looking past you.
      Verse 8 is unusual in how it happens: David's own heart says "seek his face",
      and he answers it, your face, LORD, I will seek. It reads like somebody
      overhearing their own better instinct and agreeing with it out loud. Notice
      that the next verse immediately asks God not to hide his face, so the seeking
      and the fear of being ignored are in the same breath.

## If this is your first time reading Psalm 27

Read it in one go, and watch for the moment it turns.

The first six verses are as confident as anything in the Bible. God is light,
salvation, a stronghold. Whom shall I fear? An army could camp around me and my
heart would not be afraid. There is a memory of enemies who came for him and fell
over. It ends with music.

Then verse 7 arrives and the same voice says: hear me, be merciful, answer me. Do
not hide your face. Do not turn me away. Do not leave me or forsake me. By verse 10
he is imagining his own father and mother giving up on him, and by verse 12 he is
asking not to be handed over to people who are, in his words, breathing out
violence.

Nobody smoothed that over. Whoever put this psalm together left both halves in and
did not explain the join. If you have ever meant verse 1 completely on a Tuesday
and needed verse 9 on a Wednesday, this psalm was arranged by somebody who knew
about that.

Two more things worth noticing.

Verse 4 is the centre. Given one request — one — a man with an army outside asks
not to be rescued but to be allowed to stay near God and look at him. That is a
strange thing to want in a siege, and it explains why the psalm can end the way it
does.

Which is: unresolved. The enemies are still there in verse 12, and the last verse
does not announce a rescue. It says wait for the LORD, twice, and calls that being
strong. Most people who need this psalm are in the middle of something, not at the
end of it, and the psalm declines to pretend otherwise.
