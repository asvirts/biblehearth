shortAnswer: >-
  Matthew 6 teaches people to live before God instead of performing for human
  approval. Jesus applies that principle to giving, prayer, and fasting, and he gives
  the Lord's Prayer as a simple pattern. He then addresses money, loyalty, and worry.
  The Father sees what is hidden, knows what people need, and provides daily care, so
  his kingdom can take priority. Jesus' instruction is to "seek first the kingdom"
  rather than center life on recognition, wealth, or anxiety about tomorrow.

themes:
  - name: The Father sees what others miss
    explanation: >-
      Verses 4, 6, and 18 repeat nearly the same promise: the Father sees what is done
      in secret and will reward it. Giving, prayer, and fasting can all become
      performances aimed at recognition. Jesus moves each practice away from the
      crowd and into a relationship with the unseen Father.
  - name: 'Prayer begins after "your Father already knows"'
    explanation: >-
      Jesus says the Father knows what people need before giving the model prayer.
      Prayer therefore grows from relationship and trust, not from supplying missing
      information or finding a formula that forces a response. The Lord's Prayer
      aligns the person praying with God's name, rule, provision, mercy, and protection.
  - name: Money and worry expose the same question of trust
    explanation: >-
      Treasure, service, and worry all reveal where a person expects security. Jesus
      points to an unseen Father who knows human needs and provides for creation.
      Serving God, storing heavenly treasure, and seeking the kingdom express trust
      in that care.
  - name: Jesus addresses worry without denying trouble
    explanation: >-
      Verse 34 openly says that each day has trouble of its own. Jesus does not promise
      a life free from difficulty. He asks people to meet today's responsibilities
      without carrying imagined versions of tomorrow at the same time. The Father's
      knowledge and care make that daily focus possible.

faqs:
  - q: What does each line of the Lord's Prayer mean?
    a: >-
      "Our Father in heaven" begins with relationship and shared belonging. "Hallowed
      be your name" asks that God be honored. "Your kingdom come, your will be done on
      earth as in heaven" seeks his rule here. "Give us this day our daily bread"
      asks for immediate provision. "Forgive us our debts as we forgive our debtors"
      joins mercy received with mercy extended. "Lead us not into temptation, but
      deliver us from the evil one" asks for protection through testing and evil.
      God's concerns come first, followed by the community's needs.
  - q: Does Matthew 6:15 mean God will not forgive me if I do not forgive others?
    a: >-
      Jesus treats a settled refusal to forgive as incompatible with receiving God's
      mercy. That warning differs from the slow process of working through severe
      harm with an honest desire to release vengeance. Forgiveness and reconciliation
      are also distinct. Reconciliation requires safety, truth, repentance, and rebuilt
      trust; forgiveness alone does not restore a harmful person's access.
  - q: What does "seek first the kingdom of God" mean?
    a: >-
      "Seek" means giving first attention to God's rule and the kind of righteousness he
      desires. In the surrounding verses, people pursue food, drink, and clothing;
      Jesus redirects their deepest pursuit toward the kingdom while trusting the
      Father for those needs. The promise concerns provision, not unlimited wealth.
  - q: Why would God lead us into temptation?
    a: >-
      The Greek word can refer to testing as well as enticement, while James 1 says
      God tempts no one toward evil. The prayer asks God not to bring or allow his
      people into an overwhelming test and to deliver them from evil or the evil one.
      It is closer to "do not bring us to the point of trial" than "do not try to make
      us sin". The wording "lead us not" can carry the sense "do not let us be led".
      It is a request for protection rather than a claim that God tries to make people
      sin.
  - q: Why is "for thine is the kingdom" missing from some Bibles?
    a: >-
      The earliest and strongest Greek manuscripts of Matthew omit this ending. It
      appears in later copies, probably reflecting a congregational response used in
      worship. The King James Version follows manuscripts that include it, while many
      modern translations place it in a note. The words express biblical truth and
      remain appropriate in prayer even if they were probably not part of Matthew's
      original text.
  - q: What does "you cannot serve both God and money" mean?
    a: >-
      The Greek verb translated "serve" describes service like that of a slave to an owner. Money can
      claim time, decisions, and loyalty until it functions as a master. Jesus is not
      declaring every possession evil or requiring universal poverty. He warns that
      ultimate allegiance cannot be divided between wealth and God.
  - q: Does Matthew 6:6 mean you should not pray in public?
    a: >-
      Jesus and his followers pray publicly elsewhere, and the Lord's Prayer uses
      plural words such as "our" and "us." The warning concerns prayer performed to
      gain attention. A closed room removes that audience and tests the motive. Public
      prayer remains appropriate when it is truly directed to God.
  - q: What does "do not be anxious about tomorrow" mean?
    a: >-
      Jesus acknowledges that today already has trouble and asks people to meet it
      without carrying tomorrow's imagined burdens too. Worry cannot lengthen life or
      control the future. It is narrower than "do not be afraid". Jesus says, "Do not
      worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will worry about itself. Today has enough
      trouble of its own." The instruction encourages daily dependence on the Father;
      it does not shame people with anxiety disorders or forbid prudent planning and
      professional care.

## Reading Matthew 6 as one teaching

Verses 4, 6, and 18 repeat the same promise about the Father who sees in secret.
Jesus applies it to generosity, prayer, and fasting, three respected religious
practices in his setting. Each can serve God quietly or become a performance that
receives its reward through human approval.

The Lord's Prayer comes immediately after Jesus says the Father already knows what
people need. Its purpose is relationship and alignment, not giving God information.
The prayer is brief, shared, and centered on God's honor, daily provision, mercy, and
protection.

The second half continues the question of trust. Treasure reveals the heart, money
can become a master, and worry tries to secure what the Father already knows is
needed. Jesus points to birds and wildflowers as signs of care, then asks his followers
to seek God's kingdom first and meet each day's responsibilities as they arrive.
