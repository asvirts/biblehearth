usfm: MAT.6
title: "Matthew 6: The Lord's Prayer, and the Same Sentence Said Three Times"
seoTitle: "Matthew 6: The Lord's Prayer, and One Repeated Line"
shortAnswer: >-
  Matthew 6 is the middle of the Sermon on the Mount, and it holds the Lord's
  Prayer, "you cannot serve both God and money", the birds and the lilies, and
  "seek first the kingdom". What holds all of it together is one sentence Jesus
  says three times, almost word for word, in verses 4, 6 and 18: your Father, who
  sees what is done in secret, will reward you. Giving, praying and fasting each
  get the same treatment — do not perform it, do it unseen — and then the second
  half of the chapter applies the same logic to money and worry. The subject
  throughout is living for an audience you cannot see.
outline:
  - verses: "1-4"
    heading: Giving, and the problem the whole chapter is about
    note: >-
      Verse 1 states the principle before any example: do not perform your
      righteousness to be watched. Then giving, with a joke in it — do not let your
      left hand know what your right is doing — and the first appearance of the
      sentence about a Father who sees in secret.
  - verses: "5-8"
    heading: Two ways to pray badly, and a reason not to worry about words
    note: >-
      Praying to be seen, and praying at length in the hope that volume works.
      Against the first, a shut door. Against the second, a claim that changes
      everything after it: your Father already knows what you need before you ask
      him.
  - verses: "9-13"
    heading: The prayer itself, and how short it is
    note: >-
      Fifty-odd words. God addressed as Father, then three requests about him — his
      name, his kingdom, his will — then four about us: bread for today,
      forgiveness, and rescue from testing and from evil. Nothing in it asks God to
      be informed of anything.
  - verses: "14-15"
    heading: The one line Jesus goes back and presses on
    note: >-
      Out of the whole prayer, he returns to forgiveness and states it both ways:
      forgive and you will be forgiven, do not and you will not. The hardest two
      verses in the chapter, and he leaves them without softening.
  - verses: "16-18"
    heading: Fasting, and the third repetition
    note: >-
      The same shape a third time. Do not advertise it by looking miserable; wash
      your face so nobody can tell; your Father who sees in secret will reward you.
      Notice he says "when you fast", not "if".
  - verses: "19-24"
    heading: Three pictures about where your loyalty actually sits
    note: >-
      Treasure that moths and thieves can reach against treasure they cannot, with
      the diagnosis in verse 21: your heart follows your treasure. Then an eye that
      lets in light or darkness. Then the sharpest of the three — nobody can serve
      two masters, and you cannot serve God and money.
  - verses: "25-32"
    heading: Birds, lilies, and an argument from the small to the large
    note: >-
      Jesus does not tell them to stop worrying and leave it there. He argues:
      birds get fed without farming, wild flowers are dressed better than Solomon,
      and worry has never added an hour to anyone's life. Then the plain statement
      — your Father knows that you need these things.
  - verses: "33-34"
    heading: The instruction, and an honest concession
    note: >-
      Seek first the kingdom and God's righteousness, and the rest will be added.
      Then the last verse, which does not pretend: do not worry about tomorrow,
      because today has enough trouble of its own.
themes:
  - name: The same sentence, three times
    explanation: >-
      Verses 4, 6 and 18 are nearly identical: your Father, who sees what is done
      in secret, will reward you. Jesus takes the three religious practices his
      hearers would have recognised as the marks of a serious person — giving,
      prayer, fasting — and gives each the same verdict. Doing it to be seen is not
      a smaller version of doing it for God; it is a different transaction
      altogether, and he says the people who choose it have already been paid. That
      repetition is the chapter's structure, and once you have noticed it the second
      half stops looking like a change of subject.
  - name: The model prayer comes after "your Father already knows"
    explanation: >-
      The order of verses 7 to 9 is easy to miss and it reframes the prayer. Do not
      pile up words like people who think quantity gets through, because your
      Father knows what you need before you ask. So then, pray like this. The
      prayer is given as an example immediately after a statement that God is not
      short of information, which rules out one common way of using it. It is not
      for briefing God, and it is not a form of words with power in the words.
      Whatever prayer is doing in this chapter, it is not transmitting a message to
      somebody who would otherwise be unaware.
  - name: Money and worry get the same argument as fasting
    explanation: >-
      The chapter looks like two halves — religion, then money and anxiety — and it
      is really one argument twice. Store treasure where nobody can see it. Serve a
      master nobody can see. Trust a Father you cannot see to feed you as reliably
      as he feeds birds you never see him feeding. Each is the same demand made of
      the giving, praying and fasting above: act for an unseen God rather than for
      visible returns. The word Father appears about a dozen times in these
      thirty-four verses, which is the clearest sign of what the chapter thinks the
      answer to anxiety is.
  - name: It forbids worry and concedes trouble
    explanation: >-
      The last verse of the chapter is worth reading carefully, because it stops
      short of the reassurance people expect. Do not worry about tomorrow — and
      then, today has enough trouble of its own. Jesus does not say the trouble is
      imaginary, or that a faithful person will not have any. He says there is
      enough of it already, and that borrowing tomorrow's adds nothing. That is a
      narrower and more usable instruction than "everything will be fine", and it
      is one somebody in a genuinely hard week can actually obey.
glossary:
  - term: hypocrite
    plain: >-
      The Greek word originally meant a stage actor — someone playing a part for an
      audience. Jesus uses it six times in this chapter, and the theatrical sense
      is the point: the problem is not that these people are insincere about
      everything, but that their religion has an audience and is aimed at it.
  - term: hallowed
    plain: >-
      An old English word meaning treated as holy, set apart, taken seriously as
      sacred. "Hallowed be your name" is a request rather than a compliment: it asks
      that God's name be held in the honour it deserves, starting with the person
      praying.
  - term: daily bread
    plain: >-
      The Greek adjective here is so rare that its exact meaning is uncertain —
      it may mean "for today", "for the coming day", or "enough to get by on". All
      three land in the same place: enough food for the next stretch, asked for
      one stretch at a time, which is a small request rather than a large one.
  - term: debts and trespasses
    plain: >-
      Matthew's word is debts — something owed. Luke's version of the prayer says
      sins, and the traditional English wording uses trespasses, which came in
      through older translations. All three are describing the same thing: wrongs
      that have left a balance outstanding between people.
  - term: mammon
    plain: >-
      An Aramaic word for wealth or property, kept untranslated in older English
      Bibles, which is why it can sound like the name of a god. Jesus treats money
      as a rival master rather than a neutral tool, and that is the force of the
      word: not an idol with a temple, but something that can employ you.
  - term: fasting
    plain: >-
      Going without food for a set time for religious reasons. Jesus assumes his
      hearers do it — he says "when you fast", not "if" — and his only instruction
      is to make it undetectable, which rules out the visible misery that made it
      worth doing publicly.

  - usfm: MAT.6.33
    why: >-
      The instruction the whole second half builds to, and the verse most often

    why: >-
      The chapter's last word, and the most honest thing in it. Worry about
      tomorrow is ruled out on the grounds that today already has enough trouble —
      not on the grounds that there is none.
caution: >-
  Two lines here get used to hurt people. Verses 14 and 15 say that if you do not
  forgive others, your Father will not forgive you, and that sentence has been read
  to people carrying serious harm — abuse, betrayal, violence — as a threat: forgive
  your way to peace immediately, or lose your standing with God. Two things are
  worth saying against that use. Jesus is not describing a single act of the will
  performed on command; he is describing the settled refusal of somebody who wants
  mercy for themselves and intends to withhold it from everybody else. And nothing
  in the verse requires trusting the person again, restoring access to you, or
  pretending the harm was small. Forgiveness in the New Testament is not the same as
  reconciliation, and it is very rarely quick. The other misuse is verse 33 turned
  into a formula, where seeking the kingdom first is the deposit and prosperity is
  the return. Read the sentence in its paragraph and "all these things" means the
  food, drink and clothing of verses 25 to 32 — the things birds and wild flowers
  get. It is a promise of enough, made to people who were genuinely unsure of
  enough, and it is not an investment strategy.
faqs:
  - q: What does each line of the Lord's Prayer mean?
    a: >-
      "Our Father in heaven" sets the relationship before anything is asked, and it
      is plural from the first word. "Hallowed be your name" asks that God be
      treated as holy, beginning with the one praying. "Your kingdom come, your will
      be done on earth as in heaven" asks for God's rule and wishes to arrive here,
      in the visible world. "Give us this day our daily bread" is a request for
      enough for the immediate future, not for security. "Forgive us our debts as we
      forgive our debtors" ties the two together deliberately. "Lead us not into
      temptation, but deliver us from the evil one" asks to be kept out of testing
      rather than made strong enough for it. Three requests about God, four about
      us, in that order.
  - q: Does Matthew 6:15 mean God will not forgive me if I do not forgive others?
    a: >-
      The verse says exactly that, and Jesus does not soften it, so the honest place
      to start is that he meant something serious. What he is describing is a
      settled state: somebody who asks God for mercy while holding on to the right
      to refuse it to everyone else. That posture and being forgiven are
      incompatible, which is the point. What the verse is not describing is a person
      who wants to forgive and cannot yet, or who is working through real damage
      slowly. Forgiveness in the New Testament is also not the same as
      reconciliation — it does not require trusting the person, restoring the
      relationship, or calling the harm small. If this verse is frightening you,
      that is usually a sign it is being applied to the wrong situation.
  - q: What does "seek first the kingdom of God" mean?
    a: >-
      In context it is the alternative to the worrying Jesus has just described.
      "Seek" is what the previous verses said the surrounding culture does with food
      and clothes; he is telling his hearers to aim that same energy at God's rule
      and God's kind of rightness instead, and to let the practical things follow.
      The promise attached — all these things will be added — refers to what the
      paragraph has been about: eating, drinking and having something to wear. It is
      a promise of enough rather than a promise of plenty, and treating it as a
      formula for wealth requires ignoring the eight verses it grew out of.
  - q: Why would God lead us into temptation?
    a: >-
      The line has troubled readers for a very long time, and the difficulty is
      real: elsewhere the New Testament says God tempts nobody. Two things help. The
      Greek word covers testing as well as enticement, so the request is closer to
      "do not bring us to the point of trial" than "do not try to make us sin". And
      Hebrew and Aramaic prayer often phrases a request for protection as a request
      that God not allow something — so "lead us not" can carry the sense of "do not
      let us be led". The second half of the line points the same way: deliver us
      from the evil one. It is a prayer to be spared the test, not a theory about
      who arranges it.
  - q: Why is "for thine is the kingdom" missing from some Bibles?
    a: >-
      Because the earliest and most reliable Greek manuscripts of Matthew do not
      have it. The ending appears in later copies, and it was almost certainly a
      congregational response — something the church said aloud at the end of the
      prayer — that eventually got written into the text. The King James Version
      was translated from later manuscripts and includes it; most modern
      translations leave it out of the verse and note it in the margin. Nothing
      doctrinal turns on it, and there is no reason to stop saying it. It is a true
      thing to say; it just does not appear to be part of what Matthew wrote.
  - q: What does "you cannot serve both God and money" mean?
    a: >-
      The word behind "serve" is the one for being a slave, and the sentence turns
      on it. Jesus is not saying money is dirty or that Christians should be poor.
      He is saying money is a master — something with claims on your time, your
      decisions and your loyalty — and that nobody can belong to two owners at once.
      Older Bibles keep the original word, mammon, which makes it sound like the
      name of a pagan god; the plainer point is worse. The rival is not an idol in a
      temple somewhere. It is the ordinary financial pressure that quietly sets
      your priorities.
  - q: Does Matthew 6:6 mean you should not pray in public?
    a: >-
      No, and the prayer Jesus gives four verses later is plural throughout — our
      Father, give us, forgive us — which is hard to square with a rule against
      praying together. What he attacks in verses 5 and 6 is motive, not location:
      people who loved praying where they would be seen doing it. The shut door is
      the cure for that specific disease, because a room with nobody in it removes
      the audience. Jesus himself prayed with others and in front of crowds. The
      test the passage sets is not where you are standing but whether you would
      still be praying if nobody knew.
  - q: What does "do not be anxious about tomorrow" mean?
    a: >-
      Verse 34 gives its own reason, and the reason is unusually blunt: today has
      enough trouble of its own. Jesus is not claiming tomorrow will be fine, and he
      is not describing a life without difficulty. He is pointing out that worry
      relocates tomorrow's trouble into today, so you carry two days at once and
      change nothing — which is what verse 27 already said, that no amount of it
      adds an hour to your life. The instruction is narrower than "do not be
      afraid". It is about the timeframe you are trying to manage, and it leaves you
      with one day, which is what you have anyway.

## If this is your first time reading Matthew 6

Read verses 4, 6 and 18 one after the other before you read anything else. They
are nearly the same sentence: your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will
reward you.

That repetition is the chapter. Jesus takes the three things a religious person in
his world would have been most respected for — giving money away, praying, going
without food — and says the same thing about each. Do not do it where it will be
noticed. Do it where only God can see. And then he adds the sting: the people who
do it for an audience have already been paid, in full, in applause.

Once you have that, the second half of the chapter stops looking like a change of
subject. Treasure in heaven is treasure nobody can see. God and money are two
masters, one of them invisible. The birds get fed by a Father nobody watches doing
it. It is one argument all the way through, and the word that keeps recurring is
Father — about a dozen times in thirty-four verses.

Two smaller things to watch for.

The Lord's Prayer arrives immediately after Jesus says your Father already knows
what you need before you ask him. That order is deliberate, and it rules out
treating the prayer as either a briefing or a formula. It is short — about fifty
words — and nothing in it tells God anything.

And read the very last verse slowly. "Do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow
will worry about itself. Today has enough trouble of its own." That is not a
promise that nothing bad is coming. It is a much smaller and much more practical
claim: you have one day's worth of trouble to deal with, and worrying about the
next day means carrying two.
