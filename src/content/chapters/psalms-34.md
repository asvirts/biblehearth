title: "Psalm 34: Taste and See, Written by a Man Who Escaped by Faking Madness"
seoTitle: "Psalm 34: Taste and See, and Who Wrote It"
shortAnswer: >-
  Psalm 34 is a confident psalm — I sought the LORD and he delivered me from all my
  fears, taste and see that the LORD is good — and its heading names the occasion. It
  belongs to the episode in 1 Samuel 21 where David, on the run and cornered in
  enemy territory, saved his life by pretending to be insane in front of a foreign
  king: drooling into his beard, clawing at the gate. That is the rescue he is
  celebrating, and the psalm never mentions it. It is also an alphabet poem, built to
  be taught, and it turns halfway through into a lesson: the fear of the LORD,
  explained as watching your mouth and doing good.
outline:
  - verses: "1-3"
    heading: Praise announced, and an invitation to join in
    note: >-
      Blessing God at all times, boasting in him, and then a turn to other people:
      let the oppressed hear this and be glad. Verse 3 is a summons — magnify the
      LORD with me. This is not private devotion; he is recruiting.
  - verses: "4-7"
    heading: What actually happened, told without details
    note: >-
      I sought, he answered, he delivered me from all my fears. "This poor man called
      out and the LORD heard him" — David describing himself in the third person. And
      then an image of a guard: the angel of the LORD camped in a ring around those
      who fear him.
  - verses: "8-10"
    heading: Taste and see
    note: >-
      An invitation to test the claim rather than accept it. Then two verses about
      lacking nothing, with a striking comparison — young lions, the most capable
      hunters in the region, go hungry while those who seek God do not.
  - verses: "11-14"
    heading: The psalm turns into a lesson
    note: >-
      "Come, children, listen to me; I will teach you the fear of the LORD." And what
      follows is not mystical: keep your tongue from evil, stop lying, turn from evil,
      do good, seek peace and chase it. Peter quotes this passage in his own letter.
  - verses: "15-18"
    heading: Eyes, ears, and where God stands
    note: >-
      God's eyes on the righteous and his ears turned towards them; his face set
      against those doing evil. Then verse 18, the most-loved line in the psalm: the
      LORD is near the brokenhearted, and saves the crushed in spirit.
  - verses: "19-22"
    heading: An admission, and a bone that is not broken
    note: >-
      "Many are the afflictions of the righteous" — and then, in the same sentence, he
      delivers them from all of them. Not one bone broken. And a last line about
      taking refuge, which is where the psalm started.
themes:
  - name: The occasion behind it is a man faking madness
    explanation: >-
      The psalm's heading ties it to one of the least dignified moments in David's
      life. In 1 Samuel 21 he is running from Saul, takes refuge in the Philistine city
      of Gath, is recognised as the man who killed their champion, and gets out alive by
      pretending to have lost his mind — letting spit run into his beard and scratching
      at the doors of the gate. That is the deliverance behind "he delivered me from all
      my fears". The psalm never describes it, which is the interesting part. Whatever
      "taste and see that the LORD is good" means here, it is said by somebody whose
      rescue arrived through humiliation and improvisation rather than through anything
      that looked like power.
  - name: It is an alphabet poem, built to be taught
    explanation: >-
      Psalm 34 works through the Hebrew alphabet, one letter per verse, with a single
      letter skipped. Several psalms and the poem at the end of Proverbs do the same,
      and the form does two things: it signals completeness — the subject covered from
      A to Z — and it makes memorising easier. That fits what the psalm does at verse
      11, where it stops testifying and starts teaching: come, children, listen. This
      is a poem designed to be learned by heart by people who could not read, which is
      also why its instructions are so concrete.
  - name: It promises rescue from all troubles and admits there are many
    explanation: >-
      Verse 19 is the psalm being honest with itself: many are the afflictions of the
      righteous — and the LORD delivers him from them all. Both halves are in the same
      sentence and neither is withdrawn. Verse 6 says the same thing twice over, and
      verse 18 puts God next to people who are still broken rather than people who have
      been mended. The psalm is not claiming that a faithful life is a smooth one. It
      is claiming that no trouble in it is final, which is a different and more durable
      promise, and it is the reason the psalm can be sung by somebody currently in
      trouble.
  - name: The fear of the LORD is taught as three practical things
    explanation: >-
      Verse 11 promises to teach the fear of the LORD, which sounds like it will be
      about awe or worship, and then verses 13 and 14 arrive: keep your tongue from
      evil, keep your lips from lying, turn away from evil, do good, seek peace and
      pursue it. That is the whole curriculum. Two of the five are about speech, which
      is a recurring biblical instinct about where character shows first. Peter quotes
      this exact passage in his first letter when telling Christians how to live under
      hostility, which suggests the early church read it the same way — as practical
      instruction rather than as atmosphere.
glossary:
  - term: the angel of the LORD
    plain: >-
      A figure who appears throughout the Old Testament, sometimes indistinguishable
      from God himself and sometimes clearly a messenger. Verse 7 uses military
      language for him — encamping, the way an army pitches around something it is
      guarding.
  - term: taste and see
    plain: >-
      An invitation to test something by experience rather than take it on report. The
      verb is the ordinary word for tasting food. It is one of the few places in the
      Bible where the reader is effectively told to try it and find out.
  - term: the fear of the LORD
    plain: >-
      Not being frightened of God, but taking him seriously enough to live differently
      — closer to deep respect than to terror. This psalm is unusually helpful because
      verses 13 and 14 spell out what it looks like in practice.
  - term: brokenhearted and contrite
    plain: >-
      Two words for someone who has been broken rather than someone who is merely sad.
      "Contrite" translates a word meaning crushed or shattered. Verse 18 puts God
      nearest to people in that state, which is not where most religions locate him.
  - term: magnify
    plain: >-
      To make larger — not by adding anything to God, but by making him look as large
      as he is to whoever is watching. Verse 3 asks other people to help do it, which
      is why the psalm keeps turning outward.
  - term: redeem
    plain: >-
      To buy back, as a relative buys a family member out of debt or slavery. The last
      verse uses it of God and his servants, and it implies both cost and family
      obligation.

caution: >-
  Verse 10 says those who seek the LORD lack no good thing, and verse 19 says God
  delivers the righteous from all their afflictions. Read as guarantees, both will
  fail an honest reader inside a week — and the psalm itself does not read them that
  way, since the same verse 19 opens by admitting that the afflictions of the
  righteous are many. What the psalm claims is that nothing in a life given to God is
  finally lost, not that nothing goes wrong in it. Verse 20 needs the same care.
  "Not one of them will be broken" is about bones standing for the whole person, in
  the way we still say somebody was shattered; righteous people break bones, and the
  psalm's original readers knew it. And verse 18 deserves protecting from a gentler
  misuse: it says God is near the brokenhearted, which is a statement about where he
  is, not an instruction to stop being brokenhearted. It is one of the few verses in
  Scripture that can be offered to somebody in the first week of a loss without
  asking anything of them.
faqs:
  - q: What does "taste and see that the LORD is good" mean?
    a: >-
      It is an invitation to test the claim rather than accept it on someone else's
      word. The verb is the ordinary one for tasting food, and it does what an offered
      spoonful does — you cannot know from a description. The verse sits immediately
      after David's account of being answered and rescued, so the logic runs: this is
      what happened to me, now find out for yourself. What makes it striking is the
      heading of the psalm. The experience he is inviting people to test is one where
      God's rescue came through a humiliating improvisation in front of enemies, not
      through an obvious display of power.
  - q: What does "the LORD is near to the brokenhearted" mean?
    a: >-
      It is a statement about location, and that is what makes it useful. The second
      half of the verse pairs it with "the contrite in spirit" — a word meaning crushed
      or shattered, describing somebody broken rather than merely sad. What the verse
      claims is that God is nearest to people in exactly that state. It does not tell
      them to cheer up, does not attach a condition, and does not say the breaking was
      deserved or good. It is one of the few verses that can be said to somebody in the
      worst week of their life without asking anything of them in return.
  - q: Does Psalm 34:20 predict that none of Jesus' bones would be broken?
    a: >-
      John's Gospel quotes it at the crucifixion, where soldiers break the legs of the
      two men beside Jesus and find him already dead, so they do not break his. John
      says this happened so that Scripture would be fulfilled, and this verse — along
      with the Passover instruction not to break the lamb's bones — is what he has in
      mind. Read in its own psalm, the line is about bones standing for a whole person,
      the way we still say somebody was shattered; it is a promise of preservation
      rather than an anatomical forecast. Christians generally understand John to be
      doing what New Testament writers do often: recognising a pattern completed in
      Jesus rather than claiming the psalmist knew what he was describing.
  - q: What does "the angel of the LORD encamps around those who fear him" mean?
    a: >-
      The verb is military. To encamp is what an army does when it pitches around
      something it intends to defend, so the picture is a ring of protection rather
      than a companion walking beside you. The angel of the LORD is a figure who turns
      up throughout the Old Testament, sometimes spoken of as a messenger and sometimes
      in ways that make him hard to distinguish from God himself; the psalm does not
      resolve that. What the verse offers is scale: one person on the run is described
      as having a camp around him, which is a deliberate reversal of how David's
      situation actually looked at the time.
  - q: What happened when David wrote Psalm 34?
    a: >-
      The heading connects it to the episode in 1 Samuel 21. David is fleeing Saul with
      nothing, and he takes refuge in Gath — a Philistine city, and the home town of
      the champion he had killed. He is recognised almost immediately, and the account
      says he was very afraid. So he saves himself by pretending to be insane: letting
      his spit run down his beard and scratching at the doors of the city gate. The
      king dismisses him as a madman and he gets out alive. The psalm's heading names
      the king as Abimelech, which was probably a royal title rather than a personal
      name. None of that appears in the psalm itself.
  - q: Does Psalm 34:10 promise Christians will never lack anything?
    a: >-
      Not in the sense of getting everything you want. The verse contrasts young lions
      — the most effective hunters anyone in that world could name — going hungry, with
      those who seek the LORD lacking "no good thing". The comparison is about who can
      be relied on to provide, not about a guaranteed standard of living, and the phrase
      "no good thing" leaves the definition of good with God rather than with the
      reader. The strongest argument against a prosperity reading is the psalm itself:
      nine verses later it says the afflictions of the righteous are many. A poem that
      admits that is not promising a life without shortage.
  - q: What does the fear of the LORD mean in Psalm 34?
    a: >-
      This psalm is unusually helpful on the question, because it promises to teach it
      and then does. Verse 11 says come and listen, I will teach you the fear of the
      LORD, and verses 13 and 14 give the content: keep your tongue from evil, keep your
      lips from lying, turn away from evil, do good, seek peace and pursue it. That is
      the whole syllabus, and none of it is about feeling afraid. Fearing God, in this
      psalm, means taking him seriously enough that it shows up in how you talk and what
      you chase. Peter quotes the same passage in his first letter when instructing
      Christians living under pressure.

## If this is your first time reading Psalm 34

Read the heading. Most Bibles print it in small type above verse 1, and it is the key
to the whole thing.

It ties this psalm to 1 Samuel 21, which is one of the least heroic passages in
David's story. He is on the run from Saul with nothing, and he takes shelter in Gath —
a Philistine city, and the home of the giant he had killed. They recognise him at
once. The account says plainly that he was very afraid. And the way he gets out alive
is by pretending to have lost his mind: letting his spit run into his beard and
clawing at the doors of the city gate until the king decides he is a lunatic and not
worth the trouble.

Then he writes: I sought the LORD, and he answered me; he delivered me from all my
fears. Taste and see that the LORD is good.

That is worth sitting with. The rescue he is celebrating did not look like a rescue.
It looked like a desperate man humiliating himself in front of enemies. And the psalm
does not mention any of it — it simply calls it deliverance and invites everyone else
to try God for themselves.

Two other things to watch for.

At verse 11 the psalm changes jobs. It stops being testimony and becomes a lesson:
come, children, listen, I will teach you the fear of the LORD. And what follows is not
lofty. Watch your mouth. Stop lying. Turn away from evil. Do good. Chase peace. Peter
quotes that exact passage in his own letter, to Christians living under pressure.

And read verse 19 slowly, because it is the psalm being honest. "Many are the
afflictions of the righteous — and the LORD delivers him from them all." Both halves
stay. It is not promising you a life where nothing goes wrong. It is promising that
nothing that goes wrong gets the final say, which is a claim you can actually test from
inside the trouble.
