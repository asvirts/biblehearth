shortAnswer: >-
  Psalm 34 celebrates God's care in the middle of danger. Its heading connects the
  poem to 1 Samuel 21, when David escaped from a Philistine king by pretending to be
  insane. David remembers that frightening episode as an answer to prayer and invites
  others to "taste and see that the LORD is good." Halfway through, his testimony
  becomes a lesson about fearing God through honest speech, good actions, and an
  active pursuit of peace.

themes:
  - name: David's humiliating escape becomes a story of God's care
    explanation: >-
      According to its heading, Psalm 34 comes from one of David's least dignified
      escapes. First Samuel 21 describes him fleeing Saul, entering the Philistine
      city of Gath, and being recognized as the man who killed their champion. David
      survives by acting insane, complete with spit in his beard and scratches on the
      city gate. He later describes that unlikely escape with the words "he delivered
      me from all my fears." His invitation to taste God's goodness grows out of a
      rescue marked by fear, quick thinking, and public humiliation.
  - name: The alphabet form helps listeners learn the poem
    explanation: >-
      Psalm 34 follows the Hebrew alphabet, using a new letter for each verse while
      skipping one letter. Other biblical poems use the same acrostic pattern. It
      gives the poem a sense of completeness and makes it easier to remember. That
      form suits the teaching section beginning in verse 11, where David asks his
      listeners to learn the fear of the LORD. The memorable structure carries
      practical instruction for a community accustomed to hearing Scripture aloud.
  - name: Faithful people still face many troubles
    explanation: >-
      Verse 19 holds both halves together: "Many are the afflictions of the righteous
      — and the LORD delivers him from them all." Earlier verses make
      the same pairing: David cries from distress, and God draws near to people who
      remain brokenhearted. Psalm 34 gives no picture of an easy life. It offers hope
      that trouble will not have the final word, even while a person is still living
      through it.
  - name: Fearing God shapes speech, conduct, and peace
    explanation: >-
      Verses 13 and 14 explain the fear of the LORD in everyday terms. It appears in
      truthful speech, a rejection of evil, active goodness, and the pursuit of peace.
      The emphasis on words reflects a wider biblical concern with speech as evidence
      of character. Peter quotes this passage in his first letter while teaching
      Christians how to respond to hostility, showing how early believers carried
      David's instruction into their own circumstances.

  Verses 10 and 19 express confidence in God's provision and rescue, but they do not
  guarantee comfort, wealth, or immediate relief. Verse 19 begins by acknowledging
  that righteous people experience many afflictions. Likewise, the unbroken bones in
  verse 20 picture God's preservation of the whole person; faithful people can still
  suffer physical injuries. Verse 18 should never be used to rush someone through
  grief. It promises God's nearness to the brokenhearted without asking them to hide

faqs:
  - q: What does "taste and see that the LORD is good" mean?
    a: >-
      David uses the physical act of tasting as a picture of learning through
      experience. He has just described seeking God and receiving help, so he invites
      others to discover God's goodness for themselves. The psalm's heading makes the
      invitation especially vivid. David's own experience involved a desperate and
      humiliating escape, yet he still recognized God's care within it.
  - q: What does "the LORD is near to the brokenhearted" mean?
    a: >-
      The verse assures hurting people of God's presence. The phrase "the contrite in spirit"
      deepens
      the image by describing someone who feels shattered inside. God draws close in
      that condition, with no suggestion that the pain was deserved or good. The verse
      offers companionship and hope without demanding quick recovery.
  - q: Does Psalm 34:20 predict that none of Jesus' bones would be broken?
    a: >-
      John applies this verse to Jesus' crucifixion. The soldiers break the legs of
      the two men beside Jesus, but they find Jesus already dead and leave his bones
      intact. John also draws on the Passover instruction about the lamb's bones. In
      Psalm 34 itself, bones represent the preservation of the whole person. Christians
      commonly understand John as recognizing that earlier pattern in Jesus, without
      requiring David to have known the later details.
  - q: What does "the angel of the LORD encamps around those who fear him" mean?
    a: >-
      "Encamps" is military language for setting up a protective position. The image
      surrounds a vulnerable person with the kind of defense an army would provide.
      The angel of the LORD appears elsewhere as God's messenger and is sometimes
      described in language closely associated with God. This psalm leaves that
      identity unexplained and focuses on the protection David trusts God to provide.
  - q: What happened when David wrote Psalm 34?
    a: >-
      The heading points to 1 Samuel 21. David is fleeing Saul when he enters Gath, the
      Philistine hometown of the champion he killed. Once recognized, he becomes
      afraid and pretends to be insane, scratching the gate and letting saliva run
      into his beard. The king dismisses him, and David escapes. Psalm 34 calls the
      king Abimelech, probably a royal title, while 1 Samuel names him Achish. The poem
      celebrates the rescue without retelling those details.
  - q: Does Psalm 34:10 promise Christians will never lack anything?
    a: >-
      The verse does not promise every desire or a particular standard of living. It
      contrasts young lions, capable hunters that still become hungry, with people who
      depend on God. The words "no good thing" leave the definition of good with God.
      Nine verses later, David acknowledges the many afflictions of the righteous, so
      the poem clearly allows for hardship and need.
  - q: What does the fear of the LORD mean in Psalm 34?
    a: >-
      David answers the question with practical instruction. Verses 13 and 14 connect
      the fear of the LORD with honest speech, turning away from evil, doing good, and
      pursuing peace. It describes a serious reverence for God that becomes visible in
      daily choices. First Peter later quotes the same passage while instructing
      Christians who were living under pressure.

## If this is your first time reading Psalm 34

Start with the heading, usually printed in small type above verse 1. It connects the
song to an episode in 1 Samuel 21 and helps explain David's confidence.

David is running from Saul when he seeks shelter in Gath, a Philistine city and the
hometown of the champion he had killed. People recognize him, and the account says he
is very afraid. He gets away by pretending to be insane, scratching at the city gate
and letting saliva run into his beard until the king sends him away.

Psalm 34 remembers this narrow escape as an answer to prayer. David says that he
sought the LORD and was delivered from his fears, then invites everyone listening to
"taste and see that the LORD is good."

The strange circumstances matter. God's care did not arrive as a dramatic display of
strength. David survived through a desperate, humiliating performance in front of his
enemies. The poem still calls that outcome deliverance and turns one frightened man's
experience into an invitation for the whole community.

Notice the change in tone at verse 11. David begins to teach: "Come, children, listen
to me; I will teach you the fear of the LORD." His lesson is concrete. Guard your
speech, turn away from evil, do good, and pursue peace. First Peter later uses the
same words to guide Christians facing hostility.

Verse 19 also deserves a slow reading. "Many are the afflictions of the righteous"
stands beside David's confidence that the LORD delivers them. Psalm 34 makes room for
real suffering and lasting hope in the same breath. Its comfort comes from God's
presence with people in trouble, including those whose hearts are still broken.
