title: "James 1: God Tests, God Never Tempts, and Religion Is Three Things"
seoTitle: "James 1: God Tests, God Never Tempts"
shortAnswer: >-
  James 1 does something English translations hide. The word behind "trials" in verse
  2 and the word behind "tempted" in verse 13 come from the same Greek root, and James
  spends the chapter pulling them apart. Trials arrive from outside, and he says they
  produce staying power. Temptation comes from your own desire, and he says God has
  nothing to do with it — "no one should say, God is tempting me." The chapter also
  ends by defining religion, and the definition is startlingly practical: control
  your tongue, look after orphans and widows, stay unpolluted. Not one clause of it
  is about what you believe.
outline:
  - verses: "1"
    heading: A one-line introduction with a quiet claim in it
    note: >-
      James calls himself a servant of Jesus rather than his brother, which — if this
      is the James most readers think it is — is a considerable omission. He writes to
      the twelve tribes scattered abroad, so his audience is dispersed and probably
      under pressure.
  - verses: "2-4"
    heading: Trials, and what they are said to produce
    note: >-
      "Consider it pure joy" is a calculation, not a feeling — the word is about
      reckoning something up. The reason follows immediately: testing produces
      perseverance, and perseverance finished produces maturity. He does not say the
      trial is good, only that it makes something.
  - verses: "5-8"
    heading: Ask for wisdom, and the picture of the person who cannot
    note: >-
      God gives generously and without finding fault, which is one of the most
      generous descriptions of God in the New Testament. Then the warning: the
      double-minded man is like a wave, driven wherever the wind is going, and gets
      nothing.
  - verses: "9-11"
    heading: The poor and the rich, both told to boast about the wrong thing
    note: >-
      The one in humble circumstances should boast about his high position; the rich
      man should boast about his low one. Then a picture of a flower under a hot sun —
      colour gone, plant withered — applied to a man in the middle of his career.
  - verses: "12-15"
    heading: The pivot of the chapter
    note: >-
      Verse 12 closes the trial section with a crown. Verse 13 opens the next by
      forbidding a sentence — nobody may say God is tempting them. Then the sequence:
      desire lures, desire conceives, sin is born, sin grows up, death.
  - verses: "16-18"
    heading: A God who casts no moving shadow
    note: >-
      Every good and perfect gift comes down from the Father of the heavenly lights,
      with whom there is no change or shifting shadow. Sun and moon move and their
      shadows travel. The one who made them does not.
  - verses: "19-21"
    heading: Three speeds, in order
    note: >-
      Quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to anger — and a reason attached: human
      anger does not produce the rightness God is after. Then get rid of the filth and
      accept what has been planted in you.
  - verses: "22-25"
    heading: The mirror, and the difference between glancing and looking
    note: >-
      A man looks at his reflection, walks away, and immediately forgets his own face.
      Against him, someone who looks intently into the law of freedom and keeps
      looking. The whole image turns on how hard ancient mirrors were to see anything
      in.
  - verses: "26-27"
    heading: Religion defined, in three clauses
    note: >-
      Someone who cannot bridle his tongue has worthless religion. And pure religion
      is this: care for orphans and widows in their distress, and keep yourself
      unpolluted by the world. That is the chapter's last word.
themes:
  - name: Trial and temptation are one word and two sources
    explanation: >-
      The Greek behind "trials" in verse 2 and "tempted" in verse 13 share a root, and
      James is deliberately separating them. In verses 2 to 4 and again at verse 12,
      pressure comes from outside a person and produces perseverance and maturity — God
      is comfortably behind that, and there is a crown at the end of it. In verses 13
      to 15 the pull comes from inside, out of a person's own desire, and James rules
      God out of it in the strongest terms available: God cannot be tempted and tempts
      nobody. So the chapter's answer to "is God doing this to me?" depends entirely on
      which one you are describing. Hard circumstances, yes. The pull towards doing
      wrong, never.
  - name: The mirror only works if you know what mirrors were
    explanation: >-
      Verses 23 to 25 turn on a man who looks at his reflection and forgets his own
      face, which sounds absurd until you remember that a first-century mirror was
      polished bronze. The image it gave back was dim and required effort — you had to
      angle it, hold it, look hard. That is exactly the contrast James draws. The
      forgetful hearer glances; the one who is blessed "looks intently" and keeps
      looking. Reading Scripture in this chapter is not compared to a quick check in a
      bathroom mirror. It is compared to work you have to do carefully to get anything
      out of at all.
  - name: Religion gets defined, and the definition is practical
    explanation: >-
      Verse 27 is one of the few places in the Bible where the word religion is
      defined rather than assumed, and the definition has three parts: bridle your
      tongue, care for orphans and widows in their distress, keep yourself unpolluted
      by the world. Two of those are about other people and one is about you. None is
      about doctrine, ritual or attendance. James is not arguing that belief does not
      matter — he argues about faith at length in chapter 2 — but he chooses to end
      this chapter with the most checkable possible test, aimed at the two groups in
      his world with nobody to speak for them.
  - name: Both the poor and the rich are told to boast
    explanation: >-
      Verses 9 to 11 are easy to skim and they are strange. The poor brother is told to
      exult in his high position, and the rich one in his low position. Each is being
      handed the opposite of what his circumstances say about him, and the reason given
      applies only to the second: the rich man will wither like a flower in the heat,
      mid-career, still pursuing things. The passage is not romanticising poverty. It is
      saying that a church which ranks itself by wealth has the ranking upside down,
      which is a theme James returns to and sharpens in chapter 2.
glossary:
  - term: the Dispersion
    plain: >-
      Jewish communities living outside their homeland, scattered around the
      Mediterranean world. Addressing "the twelve tribes of the Dispersion" tells you
      his readers were spread out, probably a minority wherever they were, and likely
      under some pressure.
  - term: trials
    plain: >-
      Hard circumstances that put a person to the test — persecution, poverty, loss.
      The Greek word can also mean temptation, which is exactly why James spends verses
      13 to 15 distinguishing the two.
  - term: perseverance
    plain: >-
      The word means staying under a weight rather than gritting your teeth. It
      describes remaining in place when the pressure would move you, which is why
      James treats it as something a trial can produce and nothing else can.
  - term: double-minded
    plain: >-
      Literally "two-souled", a word James may have coined. It describes a person
      divided against themselves — wanting two incompatible things at once — rather
      than someone with honest questions. The wave image in verse 6 is the picture of
      it: no direction of its own.
  - term: the Father of the heavenly lights
    plain: >-
      God as the maker of sun, moon and stars. The point of the title is the contrast
      that follows: those lights move and their shadows shift with them, and God does
      not. It is a statement about reliability, made with an image from the sky.
  - term: firstfruits
    plain: >-
      The first part of a harvest, brought to God ahead of the rest, and a sign that
      more is coming. Calling his readers a kind of firstfruits in verse 18 makes them
      the early part of something larger rather than the whole of it.
  - term: the perfect law of freedom
    plain: >-
      James's phrase for God's instruction, and the two words look like opposites. He
      means something like a rule that sets you free rather than restricts you — the
      opposite of how law is usually experienced, and a claim he does not defend so
      much as assume.
  - term: bridle
    plain: >-
      The strap and bit used to steer a horse. Verse 26 uses it of the tongue, and
      James picks the image up again in chapter 3. It implies something powerful being
      directed rather than silenced.

caution: >-
  Two verses here get used to hurt people. The first is verse 2, "consider it pure joy
  when you encounter trials", which has been read to grieving and exhausted people as
  a requirement to feel happy about what is happening. James does not ask that. The
  word he uses is about reckoning something up, a judgement rather than an emotion,
  and the joy is attached to what the trial produces — perseverance, maturity — not to
  the trial itself. He never once calls the trial good. The second is verses 6 and 7,
  where the one who doubts is compared to a wave and told not to expect to receive
  anything. That has been used to tell people their prayer went unanswered because
  their faith was insufficient, which is a cruel and quite specific misreading. The
  person James describes is "double-minded" — the word means two-souled, someone
  divided about whether they even want what they are asking for. He is not describing
  somebody who asked God for wisdom while also being frightened and uncertain, which
  is the condition of most people who have ever prayed verse 5.
faqs:
  - q: What does "count it all joy when you face trials" mean?
    a: >-
      The verb means to reckon or consider — to work something out and reach a
      conclusion — so James is asking for a judgement rather than a feeling. And the
      judgement has a stated basis in the very next verse: you know that testing
      produces perseverance, and perseverance carried through produces maturity. So the
      joy attaches to the outcome, not to the experience. James never calls the trial
      good, never suggests it should be enjoyed, and never tells anybody to pretend.
      What he says is that a Christian in a hard stretch can conclude something about
      where it is going, and that the conclusion is worth having.
  - q: Does James 1:13 mean God never tests anyone?
    a: >-
      No — it means God never tempts anyone, and the distinction is the whole point of
      the passage. The same Greek root covers both ideas, which is why James has to be
      explicit. External pressure that tests a person, he treats as something God is
      comfortably behind: verses 2 to 4 say it produces perseverance and verse 12
      promises a crown to whoever stands up under it. The internal pull towards doing
      wrong is a different thing with a different source, and verses 14 and 15 locate
      it precisely: your own desire, which lures, conceives, and gives birth to sin.
      God tests. God does not entice.
  - q: What does "ask in faith without doubting" mean?
    a: >-
      Read with verse 8, which supplies the definition. The person who "doubts" here is
      the double-minded man — literally two-souled — and the picture given is a wave
      pushed wherever the wind happens to be blowing. That is not someone with
      questions or fears; it is someone with no settled direction, who wants God's
      wisdom and also wants to keep their own way of doing things. Asking in faith
      means asking as somebody who actually intends to take the answer. Anybody who has
      prayed verse 5 while feeling uncertain has not fallen foul of verse 6; the
      chapter is describing division, not anxiety.
  - q: What does "double-minded" mean in James 1:8?
    a: >-
      The Greek word means "two-souled", and as far as we can tell James invented it —
      it appears here and once more in his letter and almost nowhere else in Greek
      literature before him. It describes a person split against themselves, holding
      two incompatible intentions at the same time. Verse 6 gives the image: a wave with
      no course of its own, moved by whatever wind arrives. And verse 8 adds the
      consequence, "unstable in all his ways", which widens it beyond prayer. It is a
      diagnosis of divided wanting rather than of imperfect certainty.
  - q: What does "every good and perfect gift comes down from above" mean?
    a: >-
      The verse claims that everything genuinely good has one source, and it makes the
      claim with an image from the sky: God is the Father of the heavenly lights, and
      with him there is no change or shifting shadow. Sun and moon rise, move and set,
      and everything they light throws a shadow that travels through the day. James is
      saying the one who made them does not do that. Notice where the verse sits —
      immediately after the passage about temptation coming from inside us. Our desires
      shift; the source of good things does not.
  - q: What does "be doers of the word and not hearers only" mean?
    a: >-
      It means the point of hearing is doing, and James illustrates it with a man who
      looks in a mirror and forgets his own face. Mirrors then were polished bronze,
      dim and awkward, so getting a clear look required effort — which is the contrast
      he draws in verse 25 with someone who "looks intently" and keeps looking. The
      forgetful hearer is not someone who disagrees with what they heard. They agreed,
      walked off, and it made no difference. James's word for that is
      self-deception: you can leave church convinced you have engaged with something
      when in fact nothing has changed.
  - q: What is pure religion according to James 1:27?
    a: >-
      Three things. Bridle your tongue, which verse 26 makes the negative test — if you
      cannot, your religion is worthless. Care for orphans and widows in their
      distress. And keep yourself unpolluted by the world. In the first century, orphans
      and widows were the two groups with no legal standing and no income, so James is
      naming the people who could do nothing for him or his readers in return. What is
      striking is what the definition leaves out: no doctrine, no ritual, no
      attendance. James is not saying those do not matter. He is choosing the most
      checkable possible test and putting it last, where it will be remembered.
  - q: What is the perfect law of liberty?
    a: >-
      James's own phrase for God's instruction, in verse 25, and the two halves of it
      look contradictory: law and liberty. He does not argue for it, he assumes it —
      that God's commands are the kind of rule that frees a person rather than
      confining them, the way the rules of a language free you to say anything rather
      than restricting what you can say. The context is about looking into it and
      continuing to look, so he is talking about something you keep consulting rather
      than a code you memorise. He uses a similar phrase in chapter 2, where he calls
      it the law of freedom and connects it to loving your neighbour.

## If this is your first time reading James 1

There is one thing worth knowing before you start, and it is invisible in English.

The word translated "trials" in verse 2 and the word translated "tempted" in verse 13
come from the same Greek root. James knows that, and he spends the chapter separating
them, because his readers were confusing them — and so does almost everybody else.

Here is how he divides it. In verses 2 to 4, and again at verse 12, pressure comes at
a person from outside: hardship, persecution, loss. James says that kind of pressure
produces staying power, that staying power produces maturity, and that there is a
crown at the end of it. He is quite happy to have God behind all of that.

Then verse 13 changes footing completely. Nobody, he says, may claim God is tempting
them. God cannot be tempted and tempts no one. The pull towards doing wrong has a
different address, and verses 14 and 15 give it: your own desire, which lures you off,
conceives, and gives birth to something that grows up into death.

So if you are asking whether God is behind what is happening to you, James wants you
to ask which thing you mean. A hard season — yes, and there is something in it. The
pull to do the wrong thing — never.

Two smaller things to watch for.

Verse 23 has a man who looks in a mirror and immediately forgets his own face, which
sounds ridiculous until you remember that first-century mirrors were polished bronze.
They were dim, and you had to work at them. That is exactly the contrast: the
forgetful hearer glances, and the person James calls blessed "looks intently" and
keeps looking.

And read the last verse, because it is the least comfortable sentence in the chapter.
James defines religion, which the Bible almost never does, and the definition is:
control your tongue, look after orphans and widows in their distress, and keep
yourself unpolluted. Two of the three are about somebody else — and specifically about
the two groups in his world who had no money, no standing, and nothing to offer him
back.
