  The Bible never gives a command for or against cremation. Burial is the
  consistent biblical practice, but 1 Corinthians 15 makes resurrection depend
  on God's transforming power, not on the condition of someone's remains. Paul
  compares the buried body to a seed and the resurrection body to what grows
  from it. Most churches now permit cremation, while some continue to prefer or
  require burial. Scripture leaves the method itself open.

faqs:
  - q: Is cremation a sin?
    a: >-
      No mainstream Christian tradition teaches that cremation is a sin. There is no
      command against it in Scripture and no passage where the question arises. The
      churches that discourage it, most firmly Orthodox and as a stated Catholic
      preference, do so because of what burial signifies and how it honors the
      body. Catholic teaching adds one
      condition worth knowing: cremation should not be chosen as a way of denying the
      resurrection of the body. That condition addresses motive.
  - q: What happens to cremated bodies at the resurrection?
    a: >-
      The same thing that happens to buried ones, according to Paul. His answer in 1
      Corinthians 15 to the question "with what kind of body do the dead come?" is a
      seed: one thing is sown and something transformed comes up. He then says
      flesh and blood can't inherit God's kingdom and that the dead will be
      changed. The resurrection body doesn't depend on the old body remaining
      intact, which matters because buried bodies eventually decompose. Christians
      have never taught that people lost at sea, destroyed in fires or burned as
      martyrs are excluded.
  - q: Does the Bible ever describe someone being cremated?
    a: >-
      Once, and it is presented positively. In 1 Samuel 31, after Saul and his sons
      are killed and their bodies hung on a city wall by the Philistines, the men of
      Jabesh Gilead travel all night, recover the bodies at considerable risk, burn
      them, and bury the bones. The narrative treats this as an act of loyalty and
      Israel's later king thanks them for it. Some readers argue the burning was of
      spices rather than the bodies, since the Hebrew is capable of it, but the plain
      reading is a cremation carried out by faithful Israelites and never criticized.
  - q: Why did early Christians insist on burial?
    a: >-
      Partly because they inherited Jewish practice, and partly as a deliberate
      contrast with the surrounding culture. Cremation was normal in the Roman world,
      while Christians buried their dead, often in catacombs, and treated martyrs'
      graves as places of honor. Early Christian writers connected this practice
      with resurrection hope and the body's future. Burial became a statement of
      belief as well as a custom in a society that found the whole idea of a
      raised body faintly ridiculous.
  - q: Is scattering ashes acceptable for Christians?
    a: >-
      It depends on your church. Most Protestant denominations have no rule, and
      scattering is common. Catholic teaching is explicit that it is not permitted:
      ashes should be kept whole in a cemetery or other sacred place, not scattered,
      divided among family members or kept at home. The reasoning is that the remains
      should have a resting place where the community can pray, and that dividing or
      dispersing them can obscure the body's dignity. Scripture never addresses
      the question, so this is a matter of church practice and conscience.
  - q: What should a Christian consider when deciding?
    a: >-
      Scripture leaves room for practical and pastoral judgment. Relevant
      questions include what the church teaches, what the deceased requested,
      what the family can afford, what grieving relatives can bear, and whether
      burial's picture of a body laid down in hope matters to the service. A
      careful decision based on these concerns doesn't require a defense as an
      act of obedience or disobedience.

## The question the Bible does not answer

No biblical verse directly addresses cremation. Scripture records no command,
prohibition, or discussion comparing it with burial. Arguments on the subject
therefore draw from biblical patterns, historical associations, or the doctrine
of resurrection. Each approach contributes something, which helps explain why
sincere Christians reach different conclusions.

Verses are often quoted as if they decide the matter, but none of them speaks to
modern cremation directly.

## What the Bible does contain

**A consistent practice.** Scripture normally records burial. Abraham buys a
field and cave for Sarah. Jacob asks his sons to return his body there. Joseph
leaves instructions about his bones that Israel fulfills centuries later. Jesus
is wrapped in linen and placed in a tomb, and devout believers bury Stephen with
deep mourning. Burial is the assumed practice in that culture.

**A few hostile burnings.** Achan is stoned and then burned. Josiah burns human
bones on pagan altars to defile them. In Amos, God indicts Moab for burning the
king of Edom's bones. This is one of Scripture's few cases where treatment of a
dead body is part of the crime.

**One loyal exception.** In 1 Samuel 31, the men of Jabesh Gilead travel all
night into enemy territory to recover the mutilated bodies of Saul and his sons.
They burn the bodies and bury the bones. Scripture presents the act as brave and
faithful, without criticism.

## The doctrine that settles the fear

Many people asking this question are concerned about resurrection, especially
after a relative has already been cremated and someone has raised a frightening
doubt.

Paul answers the underlying concern clearly in 1 Corinthians 15. Responding to a
question about the body that will be raised, he compares death and resurrection
to sowing seed. The seed placed in the ground breaks down, and what grows is
transformed. Paul says flesh and blood can't inherit the kingdom, the perishable
must put on the imperishable, and everyone will be changed.

The resurrection body in Paul's account is transformed, not merely repaired.
Its arrival doesn't depend on the old material remaining intact. Every buried
body eventually decomposes, and many faithful Christians were burned at the
stake specifically to deny them a grave. For two thousand years, the church has
honored those people as martyrs without questioning their resurrection.

## What remains to decide

Once that fear is addressed, the decision becomes more practical and pastoral.
What should the funeral communicate, and what can the family bear and afford?

Burial carries meaningful symbolism. A body laid in a marked place that loved
ones can visit expresses dignity, memory, and hope in a future resurrection.
Those are substantial reasons to choose it.

Cremation may fit what a family can manage. Cost, distance, local availability,
and what a grieving spouse can face all matter. None of these concerns is a
spiritual compromise, and none turns a respectful choice into the hostile acts
described in passages about Achan or Moab.
