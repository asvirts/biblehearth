question: What Does the Bible Say About Cremation?

shortAnswer: >-
  The Bible never addresses cremation. It has no command for it, no command against
  it, and no passage where anyone asks the question. What it does have is a
  consistent pattern — every burial it records is a burial — and a doctrine of
  resurrection that turns out not to depend on the remains at all. Paul's argument
  in 1 Corinthians 15 is that the body which rises is not the one that was put in
  the ground, any more than a plant is the seed. Most churches now permit cremation
  and some still prefer burial, and the honest reason is that Scripture leaves it
  open.

views:
  - id: burial-pattern
    name: Burial is the pattern and it means something
    claim: >-
      Every burial in Scripture is a burial, Jesus himself was laid in a tomb, and the
      New Testament's picture of resurrection is a seed sown in the ground — so
      Christians should keep the practice that carries the picture.
    support: >-
      The Bible's silence on cremation is not neutral silence; it is the silence of a
      culture in which one practice was simply assumed. Abraham buys a cave to bury
      Sarah and the purchase gets a whole chapter. Jacob and Joseph both give
      instructions about where their bones are to end up. Jesus is wrapped in linen and
      laid in a tomb, and the empty tomb is the physical evidence at the centre of
      Christian faith. Stephen is buried by devout men. And when Paul explains the
      resurrection he reaches for planting: what is sown perishable is raised
      imperishable. Burial is a slow, deliberate act that says the body is not rubbish
      and is not finished — it has been put somewhere to wait. On this reading, the
      question is not whether cremation is permitted but whether it says what a
      Christian funeral should say.
    objection: >-
      A consistent cultural practice is not a command, and the Bible contains many
      of them that nobody treats as binding — foot-washing at meals, greeting with a
      kiss, meeting in houses. The argument also proves less than it looks: if burial
      is required because it pictures the resurrection, then the picture matters more
      than the doctrine, which is the wrong way round. And the seed image cuts against
      the argument at least as much as for it, because Paul's whole point in that
      passage is that the seed and the plant are not the same body. If continuity of
      material were the issue, he would not have chosen an illustration in which the
      original visibly disintegrates.
    held: >-
      The historic and continuing preference of Orthodox churches, which generally do
      not permit cremation, and the long-standing Catholic preference — the Vatican
      lifted its prohibition in 1963 while still stating that burial is preferred. Many
      conservative Protestants take the same view without treating it as a rule.

  - id: resurrection-unaffected
    name: The method has no bearing on the resurrection
    claim: >-
      God does not reassemble the old body, and the New Testament nowhere suggests the
      condition of the remains limits what he can do — which is fortunate, because
      Christians have been burned, drowned and left unburied for two thousand years.
    support: >-
      Paul anticipates exactly this worry in 1 Corinthians 15 and answers it with a
      seed. You do not sow the plant; you sow something that dies and looks nothing
      like what comes up. He then says plainly that flesh and blood cannot inherit
      God's kingdom and that the dead will be changed — a different kind of body, not a
      repaired one. Genesis had already said that dust returns to dust, which is where
      every buried body ends up regardless; cremation shortens a process rather than
      changing its destination. And the practical evidence is overwhelming. Hebrews 11
      lists faithful people who were stoned, sawn in two and killed by the sword;
      countless martyrs were burned deliberately, sometimes precisely to deny them a
      grave. If the state of the remains mattered, the church's own martyrs would be at
      a disadvantage, and no Christian has ever believed that.
    objection: >-
      Granting that God is not limited does not settle what a Christian should choose.
      Almost nothing in Christian practice is defended on the grounds that God could
      work around it — you could say the same about baptism with water or bread and wine
      at communion, and nobody argues from divine capability to indifference there. The
      martyr argument is also doing less than it appears: something suffered is not the
      same as something chosen, and the church's willingness to honour martyrs whose
      bodies were destroyed says nothing about whether it should destroy bodies itself.
    held: >-
      The position of most Protestant denominations, which either permit cremation
      explicitly or take no position, and increasingly the practical assumption of
      Western Christians generally. Catholic teaching now permits it provided it is not
      chosen to deny the resurrection.

    claim: >-
      When fire and human remains appear together in the Old Testament, it almost
      always means judgment or desecration — which is a reason for Christians to be
      cautious about adopting it as a normal practice.
    support: >-
      Achan and his household are stoned and then burned. Burning is prescribed as a
      penalty in the law for particular offences. Josiah desecrates the shrines of
      Israel by burning human bones on their altars, and the point of the act is that
      it makes them unclean. And most striking of all, Amos records God condemning Moab
      specifically for burning the bones of the king of Edom to lime — a nation
      indicted for what it did to a dead body. Taken together, the Old Testament
      treats the deliberate burning of human remains as something done to enemies and
      to the guilty, not to one's own dead. That association did not disappear with the
      Old Testament; the early church's insistence on burial in a world that
      commonly cremated was a deliberate choice, and part of the reason was that
      Christians believed bodies had a future.
    objection: >-
      Every example here involves either punishment or deliberate insult, which is a
      completely different act from a family choosing cremation for reasons of cost,
      land or preference. Arguing from those cases to modern practice requires the
      association to carry over independently of intent, and nothing in the texts says
      it does. Amos condemns Moab for an atrocity committed against an enemy king, not
      for a method of disposal. And 1 Samuel 31 sits awkwardly in the middle of the
      argument: the men of Jabesh Gilead burn Saul's body at real risk to themselves,
      and the narrative treats it as devotion.
    held: >-
      A common argument in conservative Protestant writing on the subject, and one of
      the historic reasons for the church's long preference for burial over the Roman
      practice of cremation.

bottomLine: >-
  There is no verse to settle this, and anyone who quotes one is stretching it. The
  Bible does not mention cremation, does not forbid it, and does not command burial;
  what it records is a culture in which burial was simply what everyone did, and a
  handful of episodes where bodies were burned as punishment or insult — plus one, in
  1 Samuel 31, where Israelites burn a body as an act of loyalty. On the doctrine that
  actually matters, Paul is unusually clear and unusually reassuring: the resurrection
  body is not the old one repaired, and he chose an illustration in which the original
  visibly rots away. So nobody's resurrection is at risk. What is left is a real
  question about meaning rather than permission. Burial says something — that this
  body has been put somewhere to wait — and it is worth knowing that you are choosing
  whether to say it. Cost, land, family wishes and what your relatives will find
  bearable are all legitimate parts of the decision, and Christians who differ on it
  are not disagreeing about the gospel.

faqs:
  - q: Is cremation a sin?
    a: >-
      No mainstream Christian tradition teaches that cremation is a sin. There is no
      command against it in Scripture and no passage where the question arises. The
      churches that discourage it — Orthodox most firmly, Catholic as a stated
      preference — do so on grounds of what burial signifies and of respect for the
      body, not because they classify cremation as sinful. Catholic teaching adds one
      condition worth knowing: cremation should not be chosen as a way of denying the
      resurrection of the body. That is about motive rather than method, and it is a
      condition almost nobody choosing cremation today would fail.
  - q: What happens to cremated bodies at the resurrection?
    a: >-
      The same thing that happens to buried ones, according to Paul. His answer in 1
      Corinthians 15 to the question "with what kind of body do the dead come?" is a
      seed: you sow one thing and something else comes up, and the plant is not the
      seed reassembled. He then says flesh and blood cannot inherit God's kingdom and
      that the dead will be changed. So the resurrection body is not the old one
      repaired, and its arrival does not depend on the old one being intact — which
      matters, since every buried body eventually decomposes completely. Christians
      have never taught that people lost at sea, destroyed in fires or burned as
      martyrs are excluded.
  - q: Does the Bible ever describe someone being cremated?
    a: >-
      Once, and it is presented positively. In 1 Samuel 31, after Saul and his sons
      are killed and their bodies hung on a city wall by the Philistines, the men of
      Jabesh Gilead travel all night, recover the bodies at considerable risk, burn
      them, and bury the bones. The narrative treats this as an act of loyalty and
      Israel's later king thanks them for it. Some readers argue the burning was of
      spices rather than the bodies, since the Hebrew is capable of it, but the plain
      reading is a cremation carried out by faithful Israelites and never criticised.
  - q: Why did early Christians insist on burial?
    a: >-
      Partly because they inherited Jewish practice, and partly as a deliberate
      contrast with the surrounding culture. Cremation was normal in the Roman world,
      and Christians conspicuously did not do it — they buried their dead, often in
      catacombs, and they treated the graves of martyrs as places of honour. The reason
      given in early Christian writing is the resurrection: the body was not disposable
      because it had a future. It was a statement of belief as much as a custom, made
      in a society that found the whole idea of a raised body faintly ridiculous.
  - q: Is scattering ashes acceptable for Christians?
    a: >-
      It depends on your church. Most Protestant denominations have no rule, and
      scattering is common. Catholic teaching is explicit that it is not permitted:
      ashes should be kept whole in a cemetery or other sacred place, not scattered,
      divided among family members or kept at home. The reasoning is that the remains
      should have a resting place where the community can pray, and that dividing or
      dispersing them treats the body as less than a body. Nothing in Scripture
      addresses the question, so this is a matter of church practice rather than
      biblical command.
  - q: What should a Christian actually consider when deciding?
    a: >-
      Since Scripture does not decide it, the honest considerations are practical and
      pastoral. What your church teaches or prefers. What the person who died wanted,
      if they said. What the family can afford, which is a real and unembarrassing
      factor. What the surviving relatives will find bearable, since a funeral is for
      the living. And whether burial's picture of a body laid down to wait is something
      you want the service to say. None of these is a matter of obedience or
      disobedience, and a decision made carefully on any of them is not a decision a
      Christian needs to defend.

## The question the Bible does not answer

It is worth being clear at the start: there is no verse about this. The Bible
contains no instruction to cremate, no prohibition on cremating, and no scene in
which anybody weighs the two options. Every argument on the subject is therefore
an argument from pattern, from association, or from doctrine — and each of those
can be made honestly, which is why sincere Christians land in different places.

That is unusual enough to say plainly, because a great deal of writing on this
question implies otherwise. Verses get quoted in both directions that are not
about cremation at all.

## What the Bible does contain

**A consistent practice.** Every funeral in Scripture is a burial. Abraham buys a
field to get a cave for Sarah. Jacob makes his sons promise to carry him back to
that same cave. Joseph, dying in Egypt, gives instructions about his bones that
are still being carried out four hundred years later. Jesus is wrapped in linen
and laid in a tomb. Stephen is buried by devout men who mourn loudly. Nobody in
the Bible considers an alternative, because in that culture there was not one.

**A handful of burnings, almost all hostile.** Achan is stoned and then burned.
Josiah burns human bones on pagan altars specifically to defile them. And in Amos,
God indicts the nation of Moab for burning the bones of the king of Edom — one of
the very few places in Scripture where what was done to a dead body is itself
named as the crime.

**One exception, and it is a good one.** In 1 Samuel 31 the men of Jabesh Gilead
walk all night into enemy territory to take down the mutilated bodies of Saul and
his sons, burn them, and bury the bones. It is the bravest thing anybody does in
that chapter, and nobody in Scripture criticises it.

## The doctrine that settles the fear

Most people asking this question are not asking about custom. They are asking
whether cremation could cost somebody their resurrection — usually because a
relative has already been cremated and someone has said something frightening.

On that, Paul is about as reassuring as it is possible to be. Someone in Corinth
had asked the same sort of question, and 1 Corinthians 15 is his answer. He
compares it to sowing seed: what goes into the ground is not what comes up, and
the seed has to fall apart for anything to happen at all. He says flesh and blood
cannot inherit the kingdom, that the perishable has to be clothed with the
imperishable, that we will all be changed.

The resurrection body, on Paul's account, is not the old one mended. Which means
its arrival does not depend on the old one surviving — and that is just as well,
since every buried body decomposes completely, and a great many faithful
Christians have been burned at the stake precisely to deny them a grave. The church
has honoured those people as martyrs for two thousand years without once
suggesting they were at a disadvantage.

## What is actually left to decide

Once the fear is dealt with, the real question is smaller and more human: what do
you want the funeral to say, and what can your family bear and afford?

Burial does say something. A body laid in the ground, in a marked place you can
return to, makes a claim — that this person is not gone and not rubbish, that they
have been put somewhere to wait. That is worth wanting, and if you want it, that
is a good enough reason.

Cremation says less, and sometimes that is fine, and sometimes it is what a family
can manage. Cost is real. Distance is real. What a grieving spouse can face is
real. None of those is a spiritual compromise, and a Christian who chooses
cremation for any of them is not owed an argument about Achan.
