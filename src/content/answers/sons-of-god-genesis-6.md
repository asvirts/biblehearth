      The phrase can therefore mean something like "the divine ones," or members of
      the divine category. Genesis doesn't define it. Clear uses elsewhere refer to
      heavenly beings, while other interpreters see Seth's covenant line or
      human rulers claiming divine descent.
  - q: Are the sons of God in Genesis 6 fallen angels?
    a: >-
      That is the oldest recorded interpretation and the most common one in
      modern scholarship. Jude 6 and 2 Peter 2:4 are usually read as supporting
      it: both describe angels who abandoned their assigned place, and 2 Peter
      places that sin immediately before the flood. The main objection is
      Matthew 22:30, where Jesus says angels in heaven do not marry; defenders
      emphasize "in heaven" and say Genesis concerns angels who left. Genesis
      itself doesn't settle the question.
  - q: Who were the Nephilim in Genesis 6:4?
    a: >-
      Genesis says only that the Nephilim were on the earth in those days, and
      also afterward, and that the unions of the sons of God with human women
      produced mighty men of renown. Whether the Nephilim are that offspring or
      a separate group already present is ambiguous in the Hebrew. The
      name may derive from the root naphal, "to fall," though that is disputed.
      The Septuagint rendered it gigantes, which is how "giants" entered English
      Bibles. Numbers 13:33 mentions them again in Canaan.
  - q: Is this the same "sons of God" as in the New Testament?
    a: >-
      No. The New Testament calls believers children or sons of God in John
      1:12, Romans 8:14, and Galatians 3:26, but that is a Greek expression
      about adoption into God's family through Christ. Genesis 6 uses a Hebrew
      phrase that elsewhere denotes members of the heavenly court. The English
      wording looks the same because translators used one English phrase for two
      different idioms. The passages make different claims.
  - q: What is the 120 years in Genesis 6:3?
    a: >-
      Two readings compete. One takes it as a new cap on the human lifespan,
      which fits the shrinking ages in the genealogies that follow, though
      Noah, Shem, and Abraham all live longer than that. The other takes it as a
      countdown: a hundred and twenty years of reprieve before the flood, which
      fits the immediate context of judgment. The clause it belongs to is itself
      uncertain, since the Hebrew verb rendered "abide" or "strive" appears
      nowhere else in the Bible.
  - q: Why do study Bibles hedge on Genesis 6:1-4?
    a: >-
      Because the evidence pulls in different directions and no single reading
      answers every objection. The phrase *bene ha-elohim* supports one view,
      Jesus' words about angels raise a challenge, and the genealogies in Genesis
      4 and 5 support another reading. Genesis never pauses to explain the term.
      Study notes that present several options are reflecting the evidence
      accurately.

## The phrase itself

The Hebrew phrase behind "sons of God" in Genesis 6:2 and 6:4 is *bene
ha-elohim*. A very literal translation would be "sons of the God" or "sons of
the gods," though the Hebrew article doesn't transfer neatly into English. More
important, "sons of X" can identify a class or group. The "sons of the prophets"
around Elijah and Elisha are members of a prophetic guild, not the prophets'
biological children. On that pattern, *bene ha-elohim* can mean "divine ones"
or members of a divine category.

The phrase remains disputed because Scripture also uses related sonship
language for human beings. Most English translations preserve the ambiguity.
The King James Version, NIV, ESV, and NASB all print "sons of God." The NET adds
a lengthy translator's note explaining the options. Some Septuagint
manuscripts, including Codex Alexandrinus, make a choice in the text and read
*angeloi tou theou*, "angels of God."

The description of the women deserves equal attention. They are *benot
ha-adam*, "daughters of humankind," using the ordinary word for the human race
and the same root as Adam. Whatever the sons of God are, the sentence places
them opposite humanity in general. It doesn't explicitly contrast one human
family with another.

## Why this was ever a question

The passage is only four verses long. It reports that the sons of God saw women,
took them, and fathered children. It mentions a limit of 120 years, notes that
the Nephilim were on the earth "in those days, and also afterward," and calls
the offspring *gibborim*, mighty men of renown. Verse 5 then moves directly to
human wickedness and the flood.

The narrator seems to assume that the first readers already know the group's
identity. Because Genesis supplies no definition, every interpretation draws on
evidence outside these four verses: other uses of the phrase, the arrangement
of the surrounding chapters, ancient cultural parallels, and the record of
early interpretation. The text itself makes that wider comparison necessary.

The same verses raise a related question that depends on different evidence:
[who the Nephilim were](/answers/nephilim-in-the-bible), and whether they were
the offspring of these unions at all.

## How the reading changed

The earliest interpretations that survive all identify the sons of God as
angels or heavenly beings. The Book of the Watchers, the opening section of 1
Enoch probably written in the third century BC, expands Genesis 6 into a story
of two hundred angels who descend, take wives, teach forbidden crafts, and
father giants. Jubilees tells a related story. Josephus and Philo assume the
same reading, followed by Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, and Tertullian.

The Sethite interpretation appears later. Julius Africanus, writing in the
third century AD, is the earliest known author to mention it, and he lists it
as one of two possibilities. Augustine argues for it at length in *City of God*
15.23, while Chrysostom promotes it in the East. By about the fifth century, the
godly line of Seth had become the standard Western interpretation and remained
so through much of the modern period.

Modern critical scholarship moved back toward the heavenly-being view. In the
twentieth century, Ugaritic texts gave scholars the related Semitic phrase *bn
ilm*, "sons of El," as a name for a divine assembly. The Dead Sea Scrolls also
preserved a text of Deuteronomy 32:8 in which God divides the nations according
to the number of the sons of God, where the traditional Hebrew text reads sons
of Israel. Most critical commentaries now understand the words in Genesis 6 as
referring to heavenly beings, though many remain cautious about the role those
figures play in the full passage.

This history doesn't settle the interpretation. It shows that the question is
ancient, that serious readers have reached different conclusions, and that
their theological assumptions have sometimes influenced how they understood
the Hebrew.

## Read the ancient sources

You can compare the interpretations in these primary texts. Each records an
author's reading of Genesis, so agreement or disagreement with that author still
needs to be weighed against the passage.

- **1 Enoch 6–8**, translated by R. H. Charles: [chapter 6](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-book-of-enoch/chapter-vi)
  describes two hundred angels descending to take wives; [chapter 7](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-book-of-enoch/chapter-vii)
  describes their offspring; [chapter 8](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-book-of-enoch/chapter-viii)
  lists the crafts and practices they teach.
- **Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews 1.3.1**, translated by William Whiston:
  [the opening of chapter 3](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/josephus/complete.ii.ii.iii.html)
  presents the angelic interpretation.
- **Julius Africanus, Fragment 2**, in his [Extant Works](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0614.htm):
  Africanus favors Seth's descendants, then considers what the angelic reading
  would imply. Look for the heading “Fragment 2.”
- **Augustine, City of God 15.23**, in [Book XV](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120115.htm):
  Augustine argues that the sons of God were human beings and discusses why
  unusual stature does not establish angelic parentage. Look for “Chapter 23.”
