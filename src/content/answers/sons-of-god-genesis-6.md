shortAnswer: >-
  Genesis 6:2 names the sons of God without explaining them. Three readings
  compete: rebellious angels, the godly line of Seth, or ancient kings claiming
  divine descent. The angelic reading is the oldest on record and matches how
  the Hebrew phrase bene ha-elohim is used everywhere else it is unambiguous;
  the Sethite reading became the Western default from roughly the fifth century
  on; the kings reading is the youngest and the least attested. Nothing inside
  the four verses settles it.

  for it: it is what the phrase means everywhere else it is unambiguous, it is
  what the earliest readers on record understood, and it explains why the flood
  narrative opens here rather than three chapters earlier. Weigh the shape of
  the story and the Sethite reading has a genuine claim: Genesis 4 and 5 build
  two lines, Genesis 6 collapses them, and the sentence that follows falls on
  humanity rather than on angels. The kings reading is the youngest and the
  thinnest on attestation, but it takes "took whomever they chose" more
  seriously than either rival. Most careful readers hold one of these with the
  other two in view, and that is not a failure of nerve — it is what four verses
  of evidence will support.

faqs:
  - q: What does "sons of God" mean in Genesis 6?
    a: >-
      The Hebrew is bene ha-elohim, literally "sons of the God" or "sons of the
      gods." In Hebrew, "sons of X" routinely names a class rather than a family
      line — "sons of the prophets" means members of a prophetic guild, not
      prophets' children — so the phrase means something closer to "members of
      the divine category." Genesis never defines it. Everywhere else the phrase
      appears unambiguously it names heavenly beings, which is why many readers
      take Genesis 6 the same way; others argue it marks the covenant line of
      Seth, or human kings claiming divine descent.
  - q: Are the sons of God in Genesis 6 fallen angels?
    a: >-
      That is the oldest recorded interpretation and the most common one in
      modern scholarship. Jude 6 and 2 Peter 2:4 are usually read as supporting
      it: both describe angels who abandoned their assigned place, and 2 Peter
      places that sin immediately before the flood. The main objection is
      Matthew 22:30, where Jesus says angels in heaven do not marry; defenders
      answer that the sentence describes angels who stayed rather than ones who
      left. Genesis itself does not settle the question either way.
  - q: Who were the Nephilim in Genesis 6:4?
    a: >-
      Genesis says only that the Nephilim were on the earth in those days, and
      also afterward, and that the unions of the sons of God with human women
      produced mighty men of renown. Whether the Nephilim are that offspring or
      a separate group already present is genuinely ambiguous in the Hebrew. The
      name may derive from the root naphal, "to fall," though that is disputed.
      The Septuagint rendered it gigantes, which is how "giants" entered English
      Bibles. Numbers 13:33 mentions them again in Canaan.
  - q: Is this the same "sons of God" as in the New Testament?
    a: >-
      No. The New Testament calls believers children or sons of God in John
      1:12, Romans 8:14, and Galatians 3:26, but that is a Greek expression
      about adoption into God's family through Christ. Genesis 6 uses a Hebrew
      phrase that elsewhere denotes members of the heavenly court. The English
      wording is identical only because translators rendered two unrelated
      idioms the same way; the underlying claims have nothing to do with each
      other.
  - q: What is the 120 years in Genesis 6:3?
    a: >-
      Two readings compete. One takes it as a new cap on the human lifespan,
      which fits the shrinking ages in the genealogies that follow — though
      Noah, Shem, and Abraham all live longer than that. The other takes it as a
      countdown: a hundred and twenty years of reprieve before the flood, which
      fits the immediate context of judgment. The clause it belongs to is itself
      uncertain, since the Hebrew verb rendered "abide" or "strive" appears
      nowhere else in the Bible.
  - q: Why do study Bibles hedge on Genesis 6:1-4?
    a: >-
      Because the evidence pulls in different directions and no single reading
      disposes of all of it. The phrase bene ha-elohim points one way, Jesus's
      remark about angels points another, the genealogies of Genesis 4 and 5
      point a third, and Genesis declines to explain itself. Study notes that
      lay out the options instead of picking one are being accurate about the
      state of the question rather than evasive.

## The phrase itself

The Hebrew behind "sons of God" in Genesis 6:2 and 6:4 is *bene ha-elohim*.
Read woodenly it is "sons of the God," or "sons of the gods" — the definite
article does some work here that English cannot easily carry. What matters more
is that Hebrew uses "sons of X" to name a class rather than a lineage. The
"sons of the prophets" who appear around Elijah and Elisha are members of a
prophetic guild, not the children of prophets. On that idiom, *bene ha-elohim*
means roughly "the divine ones," members of the category to which God belongs.

That is exactly what makes the phrase contested, because the Bible uses it that
way in some places and uses similar sonship language for human beings in
others.

Most translations decline to decide. The King James, the NIV, the ESV, and the
NASB all print "sons of God" and leave the ambiguity where they found it. A few
go further in the notes than in the text: the NET keeps "sons of God" and
attaches a long translator's note laying out the options. Some Septuagint
manuscripts, including Codex Alexandrinus, resolve it outright and read
*angeloi tou theou* — angels of God.

The other half of the sentence matters just as much and gets discussed half as
often. The women are *benot ha-adam*, daughters of humankind: the ordinary word
for the human race, the same root as Adam. Whatever the sons of God turn out to
be, the verse sets them opposite humanity in general rather than opposite one
family within it.

## Why this was ever a question

The passage is four verses long and explains nothing. It reports that the sons
of God saw, took, and fathered. It records a limit of a hundred and twenty
years. It notes that the Nephilim were on the earth in those days and also
afterward, and that the offspring were *gibborim*, mighty men of renown. Then
it moves straight to the verdict of 6:5 and the flood.

The narrator plainly assumes his readers already know who the sons of God are.
He is writing for people who need no gloss. Every reading of the passage
therefore has to be built partly from outside it — from how the phrase behaves
elsewhere, from what the surrounding chapters set up, from what the earliest
readers took it to mean. That is not a defect in the interpreters. It is the
situation the text puts them in.

The same four verses raise a second question that is usually asked alongside
this one and answered on different evidence:
[who the Nephilim were](/answers/nephilim-in-the-bible), and whether they were
the offspring of these unions at all.

## How the reading changed

The oldest interpretations we have are unanimous, and they are angelic. The
Book of the Watchers — the opening section of 1 Enoch, probably third century
BC — expands Genesis 6 into a full narrative in which two hundred angels
descend, take wives, teach forbidden crafts, and father giants. Jubilees tells
a version of the same story. Josephus states it as a matter of course. Philo
reads it that way. So do Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, and Tertullian.

The Sethite reading arrives later. Julius Africanus, writing in the third
century AD, is the earliest author known to mention it, and he mentions it as
one option of two. It became the majority Western view over the next two
centuries, carried by Augustine — who argues it at length in *City of God*
15.23 — and by Chrysostom in the East. From roughly the fifth century until the
modern period, "the godly line of Seth" was simply what the passage was taken
to mean, and the angelic reading survived mainly as an embarrassment to be
explained away.

Modern scholarship largely reversed that again. The recovery of the Ugaritic
texts in the twentieth century put *bn ilm*, "sons of El," in front of scholars
as a standing phrase for a divine assembly in a closely related Semitic
language. The Dead Sea Scrolls supplied a Hebrew text of Deuteronomy 32:8 that
divides the nations by the number of the sons of God rather than the sons of
Israel. Most critical commentaries now treat the angelic reading as the sense
of the words, while remaining considerably more cautious about what the passage
as a whole is doing.

What none of that history establishes is a verdict. It establishes that the
question is old, that serious readers have landed in different places, and that
where they landed usually had as much to do with what they thought was
theologically tolerable as with what they thought the Hebrew meant.
