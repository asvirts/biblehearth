## The name

Most modern Bibles transliterate *Nephilim* instead of translating it because
the meaning is uncertain. The usual proposal connects the word to the Hebrew
root *naphal*, "to fall." That could mean people who have fallen or people who
fall upon others. Both senses are possible, but neither is defined in Scripture.
Another proposal traces the word to an Aramaic term for a giant.

The translation "giants" is ancient. The Septuagint used *gigantes*, and the
King James Version followed that tradition. In Greek mythology, *gigantes* were
earth-born offspring of gods who fought the Olympians; the word carried more
than a simple claim about height. Its use therefore reflects an interpretation
of Genesis 6:4 as mixed parentage. English translations that print "giants"
inherit some of that interpretive history.

## The two verses

Genesis 6:4 says the Nephilim were on the earth in those days, and also
afterward, when the sons of God went in to the daughters of humankind, and
that those unions produced the *gibborim*, the mighty men who were of old, men
of renown. Numbers 13:33 has the scouts returning from Canaan reporting that
they saw the Nephilim there, that the descendants of Anak come from the
Nephilim, and that they themselves felt like grasshoppers.

Those two sentences make up the entire biblical record. One sits within a
scouting report that the surrounding narrative treats as a failure of courage.
Every fuller account draws inferences from these verses or imports details from
another source.

Genesis 6:4 says the Nephilim were present but never calls them the offspring.
The reader must decide whether the mighty men in the final clause are the same
group named at the beginning, and the Hebrew allows either conclusion. The
answer depends heavily on
[who you think the sons of God were](/answers/sons-of-god-genesis-6), and that
question is argued on evidence of its own.

## The company they keep

Whatever the Nephilim were, the Hebrew Bible surrounds them with named peoples
and ordinary geography. The Anakim are descendants of Anak living around
Hebron, and the scouting account names three of them. The
Rephaim are a category that gathers several groups: Deuteronomy 2 says the
Moabites called one of them Emim and the Ammonites called another Zamzummim,
and that both were reckoned Rephaim, tall as the Anakim. Og of Bashan is
described as the last of that remnant, remembered for an iron bed or
sarcophagus nine cubits long.

These peoples have territories, kings, and eventually defeats. Joshua's
campaign leaves Anakim only in Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod. Generations later,
Goliath comes from Gath. The narrative describes these groups through the
ordinary categories of peoples, places, warfare, and survival.

## Where the rest of it comes from

The detailed Nephilim of popular imagination are largely the giants of the Book
of the Watchers, which expands Genesis 6 into a full narrative: angels descend,
take wives, teach metallurgy and cosmetics and sorcery, and father giants who
consume everything and turn on humanity until the earth cries out. Jubilees
tells a compatible version. Josephus mentions the tradition and compares the
offspring to the Greek giants. Several deuterocanonical books, including Sirach,
Baruch, and Wisdom, refer to the giants of the old world as a settled fact
their readers would recognize.

These works aren't part of Genesis, but they belong to the interpretive world
known by New Testament writers. Jude can quote a prophecy of Enoch to a
Christian audience and expect readers to recognize it. The literature provides
valuable evidence for how ancient Jewish readers filled Genesis 6:4's gaps. It
can't establish that their reconstruction was correct.
