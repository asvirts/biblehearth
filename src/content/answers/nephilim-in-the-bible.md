shortAnswer: >-
  The Nephilim appear in just two verses — Genesis 6:4 and Numbers 13:33 — and
  the Bible never defines them. The Septuagint translated the name as giants,
  which is how the word entered English Bibles, but the Hebrew probably means
  something closer to fallen ones. Whether they were the hybrid offspring of the
  sons of God, a formidable people the verse merely dates, or simply the violent
  men of a violent age turns on how you read one difficult sentence.

  The safest thing to say is what the two verses say: the Nephilim were a group
  the Bible's first readers evidently recognised, associated with the years
  before the flood and again with Canaan, and remembered as formidable. Past
  that, an answer about who they were is downstream of an answer about who the
  sons of God were — a different question, argued on different evidence. If the
  sons of God are divine beings, the hybrid reading follows almost by itself. If
  they are Sethites or kings, it cannot follow at all. That dependency is worth
  noticing, because most confident claims about the Nephilim turn out to be
  confident claims about Genesis 6:2 wearing a different hat.

    a: >-
      Genesis 6:4 says they were on the earth in those days "and also
      afterward," and Numbers 13:33 puts them in Canaan centuries later, so the
      question is real. Three answers circulate: that a second incursion
      happened after the flood; that the Nephilim were never the flood's target
      because they were a people rather than a hybrid; or that the scouts in
      Numbers were exaggerating. The popular idea that one of Noah's
      daughters-in-law carried the bloodline has no basis in any biblical text.

      others read the sense actively, as those who fall upon others, which would
      make it a word for raiders. No verse defines it, which is precisely why

  - q: Are the Anakim and Rephaim the same as the Nephilim?
    a: >-
      Numbers 13:33 links the descendants of Anak to the Nephilim, and
      Deuteronomy 2 groups the Anakim, the Emim, and the Zamzummim together as
      Rephaim under different regional names. So the Bible relates them, but it
      never says they are identical, and Rephaim in particular does double duty
      elsewhere as a word for the dead. The likeliest reading is that Anakim and
      Rephaim were actual peoples whom Israelite tradition associated with an
      older, half-remembered reputation.
  - q: Was Goliath a Nephilim?
    a: >-
      The Bible never says so. Goliath is a Philistine champion from Gath, one
      of three cities where Joshua 11:22 says Anakim remained, which is the only
      thread connecting him to any of this. His height is given as six cubits
      and a span in the traditional Hebrew text — around nine feet — but the
      Dead Sea Scrolls, the Septuagint, and Josephus all read four cubits and a
      span, closer to six feet nine. Either way, 1 Samuel treats him as a man.

    a: >-

      is the tradition the Nephilim story belongs to, but neither writer

## The name

*Nephilim* is not translated in most modern Bibles; it is transliterated, which
is a translator's way of admitting the meaning is uncertain. The usual proposal
derives it from the Hebrew root *naphal*, "to fall," giving "fallen ones" —
either in the sense of having fallen from somewhere or of falling upon other
people. Both are defensible. Neither is stated anywhere in Scripture, and a
competing suggestion traces the word to an Aramaic term for a giant instead.

"Giants" is a translation decision, and an old one. The Septuagint rendered
*nephilim* as *gigantes*, and the King James Version followed. But *gigantes*
in Greek did not primarily mean very tall men — it named the earth-born
offspring of gods who warred against the Olympians. The choice therefore
carries an interpretation inside it: whoever made it already understood Genesis
6:4 as a story about mixed parentage. Every English Bible that prints "giants"
inherits that reading whether or not its translators intended to endorse it.

## The two verses

Genesis 6:4 says the Nephilim were on the earth in those days, and also
afterward, when the sons of God went in to the daughters of humankind — and
that those unions produced the *gibborim*, the mighty men who were of old, men
of renown. Numbers 13:33 has the scouts returning from Canaan reporting that
they saw the Nephilim there, that the descendants of Anak come from the
Nephilim, and that they themselves felt like grasshoppers.

That is the whole biblical dossier. Two verses, one of them inside a report the
surrounding narrative treats as a failure of courage. Everything else that gets
said about the Nephilim is either inference from these two sentences or import
from somewhere else.

Note also what Genesis 6:4 withholds. It does not say the Nephilim were the
offspring; it says they were present. The reader has to decide whether the
mighty men of the last clause are the Nephilim of the first, and the Hebrew
allows either answer. Which one you pick depends heavily on
[who you think the sons of God were](/answers/sons-of-god-genesis-6), and that
question is argued on evidence of its own.

## The company they keep

Whatever the Nephilim were, the Hebrew Bible surrounds them with named peoples
rather than with monsters. The Anakim are descendants of Anak, living around
Hebron, with three of them named individually in the scouting account. The
Rephaim are a category that gathers several groups: Deuteronomy 2 says the
Moabites called one of them Emim and the Ammonites called another Zamzummim,
and that both were reckoned Rephaim, tall as the Anakim. Og of Bashan is
described as the last of that remnant, remembered for an iron bed or
sarcophagus nine cubits long.

These peoples have territories, kings, and eventually defeats. Joshua's
campaign leaves no Anakim in Israel except in Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod — and it
is from Gath, generations later, that Goliath comes. That thread runs entirely
through ordinary historical narrative. It never once passes through the
supernatural.

## Where the rest of it comes from

The detailed Nephilim of popular imagination are largely the giants of the Book
of the Watchers, which expands Genesis 6 into a full narrative: angels descend,
take wives, teach metallurgy and cosmetics and sorcery, and father giants who
consume everything and turn on humanity until the earth cries out. Jubilees
tells a compatible version. Josephus mentions the tradition and compares the
offspring to the Greek giants. Several deuterocanonical books — Sirach, Baruch,
and Wisdom among them — refer to the giants of the old world as a settled fact
their readers would recognise.

None of that is Genesis, and none of it is fringe either. It is the
interpretive world the New Testament writers lived in, which is why Jude can
quote the prophecy of Enoch to a Christian audience and expect it to land. The
honest position is that this literature tells us a great deal about how ancient
readers filled the gap Genesis 6:4 leaves open, and nothing about whether they
filled it correctly.
