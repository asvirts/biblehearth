shortAnswer: >-
  Scripture takes family seriously enough to build obligations around it —
  honouring parents is among the Ten Commandments, and failing to provide for
  relatives draws unusually sharp language from Paul. It also relativises it.
  Jesus, told his mother and brothers were outside, asked who his mother and
  brothers were, and answered with whoever does God's will.

misuse: >-
  Joshua 24:15 is printed on a great deal of home decor, which obscures that it
  is a challenge issued at a national assembly where Joshua is telling Israel to
  choose and stating his own household's answer. Read as decoration it loses its
  edge. The larger error is making family an absolute. Scripture assigns it real
  obligations, but Jesus repeatedly puts loyalty to God above blood, and the New
  Testament applies household language to a church of unrelated people. Used
  carelessly, "honour your father and mother" also becomes a demand that adult
  children tolerate abuse; honouring a parent has never in Scripture meant
  submitting to harm, and the same texts hold parents to account for provoking
  and embittering their children.
faqs:
  - q: What does the Bible say about honoring your parents?
    a: >-
      Exodus 20:12 makes it one of the Ten Commandments and attaches a promise
      to it, and Paul repeats it in Ephesians 6. In context it concerns adult
      children caring for ageing parents as much as childhood obedience —
      1 Timothy 5:8 treats failure to provide for one's own relatives in
      unusually severe terms.
  - q: Did Jesus say family is not important?
    a: >-
      No, but he refused to make it ultimate. In Mark 3:33-35, told his mother
      and brothers were asking for him, he redefined family around whoever does
      God's will. He also upheld the command to honour parents and criticised
      religious workarounds that let people avoid supporting theirs. The claim
      is about ranking, not dismissal.

Two things run alongside each other here and neither cancels the other.
Scripture assigns family unusually concrete duties — provision, honour, care
across generations — and Scripture refuses to treat family as the highest
loyalty.

Jesus does both in the same gospel: upholding the command to honour parents,
and asking, with his mother waiting outside, who his mother really is.
