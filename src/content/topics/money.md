shortAnswer: >-
  The famous verse is a misquotation. 1 Timothy says the *love* of money is a
  root of all kinds of evil, not that money is the root of all evil — and the
  distinction runs through the whole subject. Scripture treats wealth as
  genuinely dangerous because of what it does to attachment and trust, while
  giving practical, unromantic advice about debt, work, and generosity.

misuse: >-
  Two misuses dominate. The first is quoting "money is the root of all evil,"
  which is not what 1 Timothy 6:10 says — it names the love of money, and calls
  it a root of all kinds of evil. The second is the prosperity reading, in which
  Malachi 3:10 and Philippians 4:19 become a mechanism for generating wealth.
  Malachi addresses a nation withholding the temple tithe; Philippians was
  written from prison to people who had just given sacrificially. Neither is a
  formula. Scripture also never treats poverty as evidence of sin or of weak
  faith — Jesus was poor, and the prophets reserve their sharpest language for
  those who exploit the poor rather than for the poor themselves.
faqs:
  - q: Does the Bible say money is the root of all evil?
    a: >-
      No. 1 Timothy 6:10 says the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil.
      Both qualifiers matter — it is the attachment rather than the currency,
      and it is one root among others rather than the single source of
      everything wrong. The same letter goes on to instruct wealthy believers
      how to hold wealth rather than telling them to be rid of it.
  - q: What does the Bible say about debt?
    a: >-
      Proverbs 22:7 observes that the borrower becomes servant to the lender,
      which is a statement about the power debt transfers rather than an
      outright prohibition. Scripture regulates lending heavily — forbidding
      interest that exploits the poor and mandating periodic release of debts —
      and treats repayment as an obligation.
  - q: Does the Bible require Christians to tithe?
    a: >-
      Tithing is commanded in the Mosaic law, and Malachi 3:10 is addressed to
      a nation withholding it from the temple. The New Testament never restates
      it as a fixed percentage; instead 2 Corinthians 9:7 asks for giving
      decided in advance, without reluctance or compulsion. Many Christians use
      ten percent as a benchmark rather than a rule.

Scripture is far less squeamish about money than its reputation suggests, and
far more pointed. It gives practical advice about debt and saving without
moralising, and it reserves its warnings for what money does to the person
holding it.

The verse everyone knows says the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil.
Dropping the two qualifiers turns a precise diagnosis into a slogan.
