shortAnswer: >-
  First Timothy says the *love* of money is *a* root of all kinds of evil. The
  familiar saying that money itself is the root of every evil drops both
  qualifications. Scripture focuses on the loyalties wealth can create and
  gives practical guidance about debt, work, contentment, and generous giving.

misuse: >-
  Two misuses dominate. The first is quoting "money is the root of all evil,"
  though 1 Timothy 6:10 names the love of money as a root of all kinds of evil.
  The second is the prosperity reading, in which
  Malachi 3:10 and Philippians 4:19 become a mechanism for generating wealth.
  Malachi addresses a nation withholding the temple tithe; Philippians was
  written from prison to people who had just given sacrificially. Neither is a
  formula. Scripture also never treats poverty as evidence of sin or of weak
  faith. Jesus was poor, and the prophets reserve their sharpest language for
  people who exploit those in poverty.
faqs:
  - q: Does the Bible say money is the root of all evil?
    a: >-
      No. 1 Timothy 6:10 says the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil.
      Both qualifiers matter. Paul addresses attachment to wealth and identifies
      one source among many kinds of wrongdoing. The same letter later tells
      wealthy believers to resist arrogance, place their hope in God, and be
      generous.
  - q: What does the Bible say about debt?
    a: >-
      Proverbs 22:7 observes that the borrower becomes servant to the lender,
      describing the power debt transfers without giving an outright
      prohibition. Scripture regulates lending heavily. It forbids interest
      that exploits the poor and mandates periodic release of debts,
      and treats repayment as an obligation.
  - q: Does the Bible require Christians to tithe?
    a: >-
      Tithing is commanded in the Mosaic law, and Malachi 3:10 is addressed to
      a nation withholding it from the temple. The New Testament never restates
      it as a fixed percentage. Second Corinthians 9:7 asks for giving
      decided in advance, without reluctance or compulsion. Many Christians use
      ten percent as a voluntary benchmark.

Scripture speaks directly about money. Its practical observations cover debt,
steady saving, work, provision, and giving. Its sharper warnings ask what money
does to the loyalties and trust of the person who holds it.

First Timothy's familiar verse is carefully qualified: the *love* of money is
*a* root of all kinds of evil. Keeping those words restores the precision that
the popular slogan loses.
