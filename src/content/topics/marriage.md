shortAnswer: >-
  Scripture describes marriage as a covenant that makes two people one
  household, and its instructions to spouses are strikingly mutual — Ephesians
  5 opens with submitting to one another, then spends three times as many words
  on what husbands owe as on what wives do. Read whole, the passage sets a
  standard of self-giving rather than authority.

misuse: >-
  Ephesians 5 is the most misused passage in this collection. It is routinely
  quoted from verse 22, skipping verse 21's instruction that everyone submit to
  one another, and stopping before the far longer and costlier command to
  husbands. Used that way it becomes a warrant for control, which is the
  opposite of a passage whose standard is self-sacrifice. More seriously, these
  verses are used to keep people in abusive marriages. Nothing in Scripture
  requires anyone to remain in danger; Peter explicitly ties a husband's
  mistreatment of his wife to his prayers being hindered, and the prophets
  treat covenant-breaking as the offence of the one who breaks it. If you are
  being harmed, these passages are not an instruction to stay.
faqs:
  - q: What does Ephesians 5 actually say about marriage?
    a: >-
      It opens in verse 21 by telling the whole church to submit to one another
      out of reverence for Christ, then addresses wives briefly and husbands at
      roughly three times the length. The standard set for husbands is Christ
      giving himself up for the church — self-sacrifice, not authority. Quoting
      from verse 22 onward reverses the passage's emphasis.
  - q: What does "the two shall become one flesh" mean?
    a: >-
      Genesis 2:24 describes a new household formed by leaving one's parents
      and being joined to a spouse, with one flesh naming the resulting union —
      physical, but also economic and social. Jesus cites it in Mark 10 to argue
      that marriage is not casually dissolved, and Paul uses it in Ephesians as
      a picture of Christ and the church.

The household passages in the New Testament read very differently depending on
where you start. Beginning at "wives, submit" produces one text. Beginning one
verse earlier, at "submit to one another," produces another.

The second is what Paul wrote. Everything that follows sits under it, including
the much longer paragraph telling husbands the measure of their love is a man
giving up his life.
