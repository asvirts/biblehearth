shortAnswer: >-
  Scripture describes marriage as a covenant that makes two people one
  household. Its instructions give responsibilities to both spouses. Ephesians
  5 begins with mutual submission, then devotes about three times as many words
  to the responsibility of husbands. Read from the beginning, the passage
  centers self-giving love.

misuse: >-
  Ephesians 5 is the most misused passage in this collection. It is routinely
  quoted as "wives, submit", skipping verse 21's instruction to "submit to one another,"
  and stopping before the far longer and costlier command to
  husbands. That fragment can become a warrant for control even though the
  passage sets self-sacrifice as its standard. More seriously, these
  verses are used to keep people in abusive marriages. Nothing in Scripture
  requires anyone to remain in danger; Peter explicitly ties a husband's
  mistreatment of his wife to his prayers being hindered, and the prophets
  treat covenant-breaking as the offense of the one who breaks it. If you are
  being harmed, these passages are not an instruction to stay.
faqs:
  - q: What does Ephesians 5 say about marriage?
    a: >-
      It opens in verse 21 by telling the whole church to submit to one another
      out of reverence for Christ, then addresses wives briefly and husbands at
      roughly three times the length. The standard set for husbands is Christ
      giving himself up for the church. Quoting only from verse 22 leaves out
      the mutual frame and the longest instruction.
  - q: What does "the two shall become one flesh" mean?
    a: >-
      Genesis 2:24 describes a new household formed by leaving one's parents
      and being joined to a spouse. "One flesh" names a union that is physical,
      economic, and social. Jesus cites it in Mark 10 to argue
      that marriage is not casually dissolved, and Paul uses it in Ephesians as
      a picture of Christ and the church.

The opening of Ephesians 5:21 matters for everything that follows. Paul first
tells the church to submit to one another out of reverence for Christ, then
addresses wives and husbands within that shared frame.

His longer instruction to husbands measures love by Christ giving himself for
the church. The passage therefore places power under the demands of mutual care
and self-giving love.
