shortAnswer: >-
  The Bible speaks of anxiety as a burden that can be brought to God.
  Philippians 4:6 pairs its instruction with practical steps: pray, ask, and
  give thanks. It then promises peace beyond what the mind can explain. The
  Psalms also give dread an honest voice, taking anxiety seriously while
  pointing anxious people toward care and companionship.

misuse: >-
  These verses don't promise that anxiety will disappear on command, and they
  aren't a diagnosis. Philippians 4:6 is sometimes quoted as if anxiety were
  simple disobedience, though Paul wrote the letter to encourage a church he
  loved. Clinical anxiety can have physiological causes, and prayer isn't a
  substitute for professional care. Nothing in Scripture discourages seeking
  help; Paul even recommends treatment for a physical ailment elsewhere in his
  letters. The laments show that faithful people may pray and still feel
  afraid. These passages offer a practice of returning burdens to God whenever
  they return. They aren't a test of whether someone has enough faith.
faqs:
  - q: What is the most quoted Bible verse about anxiety?
    a: >-
      Philippians 4:6-7 is the most frequently cited passage on anxiety. It
      tells readers to bring every concern to God through prayer, petition, and
      thanksgiving. Verse 7 then promises a peace that surpasses understanding
      and guards the heart and mind, so the promise is essential to the
      passage's meaning.
  - q: Does the Bible say anxiety is a sin?
    a: >-
      Scripture tells people not to remain consumed by anxiety, but it doesn't
      reduce the experience to simple disobedience. The Bible also preserves
      psalms of open dread and records Jesus in anguish before his arrest. Its
      recurring response is an invitation to bring worry to God, not a verdict
      against the person who feels it.
  - q: What did Jesus say about worry?
    a: >-
      In Matthew 6:25-34, part of the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus argues that
      worry adds nothing to a life and points to birds and wildflowers as
      evidence of ordinary provision. He closes by narrowing the horizon to a
      single day, telling listeners not to worry about tomorrow.

Anxiety in Scripture always has a setting. It appears during exile, betrayal,
poverty, and unfamiliar responsibility, and the responses are just as concrete.
People are given something to do with their fear and someone to bring it to.

Context matters when reading these verses. A command on its own may sound like
a rebuke. Within a letter from prison, a charge to a nervous successor, or a
psalm written during crisis, it becomes part of the company Scripture offers to
people who are afraid.
