shortAnswer: >-
  Scripture treats anxiety less as a sin to be scolded and more as a weight to
  be handed over. The command not to be anxious in Philippians 4:6 arrives
  attached to an instruction — pray, ask, give thanks — and a promise of peace
  that outruns understanding. Alongside those commands sit psalms that name
  dread plainly, so the Bible neither dismisses anxiety nor leaves it with you.

misuse: >-
  These verses are not a promise that anxiety will lift on command, and they
  are not a diagnosis. Philippians 4:6 is often quoted at people as though
  anxiety were simple disobedience, which is a misuse of a letter written to
  encourage a church Paul loved. Clinical anxiety has physiological causes that
  prayer is not intended to replace, and nothing in Scripture discourages
  seeking help — Paul recommends treatment for a physical ailment in the same
  body of letters. The laments exist precisely because faithful people have
  prayed and still felt afraid. Read these passages as an invitation to hand
  something over repeatedly, not as a test you have failed if the feeling
  returns.
faqs:
  - q: What is the most quoted Bible verse about anxiety?
    a: >-
      Philippians 4:6-7 is the most frequently cited passage on anxiety. It
      instructs readers not to be anxious about anything, but instead to bring
      requests to God through prayer and thanksgiving, promising a peace that
      surpasses understanding. It is usually quoted with verse 7 attached
      because the promise, not the prohibition, is the point of the sentence.
  - q: Does the Bible say anxiety is a sin?
    a: >-
      Scripture commands against anxiety but does not treat it as simple
      disobedience. The same collection preserves psalms of open dread, and
      records Jesus in anguish before his arrest. The consistent pattern is a
      call to transfer worry rather than a verdict on the person feeling it.
  - q: What did Jesus say about worry?
    a: >-
      In Matthew 6:25-34, part of the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus argues that
      worry adds nothing to a life and points to birds and wildflowers as
      evidence of ordinary provision. He closes by narrowing the horizon to a
      single day, telling listeners not to worry about tomorrow.

Anxiety in Scripture is rarely addressed in the abstract. It shows up attached
to circumstances — exile, betrayal, poverty, an unfamiliar command — and the
responses are correspondingly concrete. The pattern that emerges across both
testaments is not "stop feeling this" but "do something with it, and here is
who to give it to."

That pattern is worth holding onto when these verses are quoted at you. Read as
isolated commands they can sound like a rebuke. Read in their settings — a
letter from prison, a charge to a nervous successor, a psalm written mid-crisis
— they read as company.
