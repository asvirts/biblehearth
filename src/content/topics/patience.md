shortAnswer: >-
  Patience appears in Scripture as something produced rather than possessed — a
  fruit of the Spirit in Galatians, an outcome of testing in James. It covers
  two distinct things: waiting through circumstances that will not move, and
  bearing with people who are difficult. God's own patience is repeatedly given
  as both the model and the reason.

misuse: >-
  Patience is one of the easiest virtues to weaponise. Telling someone to be
  patient with injustice is not what these texts do — the same Scriptures
  contain prophets who denounce exploitation immediately and psalms that demand
  God act now. Patience in the New Testament is largely about bearing with
  people's ordinary failings and about waiting for God, not about tolerating
  harm. "Wait on the Lord" is likewise not a call to passivity: Isaiah 40 pairs
  waiting with renewed strength for continued activity, and Psalm 37 sets
  stillness specifically against anxious fretting rather than against action.
  Nor is impatience a sign of weak faith — the Psalms are full of people asking
  how long, and those prayers are preserved as Scripture.
faqs:
  - q: Is patience a fruit of the Spirit?
    a: >-
      Yes. Galatians 5:22-23 lists patience among the fruit of the Spirit
      alongside love, joy, peace, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness
      and self-control. The agricultural image is deliberate — fruit is grown
      over time rather than produced on demand, which frames patience as
      something cultivated rather than a personality trait you either have or
      lack.
  - q: What does the Bible say about waiting on God?
    a: >-
      Isaiah 40:31 promises renewed strength to those who wait, and Psalm 37:7
      pairs being still before God with not fretting over injustice. Both frame
      waiting as active and expectant rather than passive. Romans 8:25 ties
      patience directly to hope for what is not yet seen, which is what keeps
      waiting from collapsing into resignation.

Two different things travel under one word here. One is waiting — for an
answer, a change, a promise. The other is bearing with people, which is the
form that shows up most often in the New Testament's practical instructions.

Both are described as grown rather than summoned. That is the useful part: if
patience is fruit, its absence today is not a verdict on your character.
