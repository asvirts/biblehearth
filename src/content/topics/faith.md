shortAnswer: >-
  Hebrews defines faith as confidence about what is not yet seen, which makes
  uncertainty part of the definition rather than evidence against it. Scripture
  presents it as received rather than generated — a gift, in Ephesians — and
  repeatedly records people who believed and doubted in the same breath. What
  it consistently rejects is faith that stays purely internal.

misuse: >-
  Matthew 17:20 is the most damaging misquotation here. The mustard seed is an
  image of how little faith is needed, but it gets used to tell people their
  prayer failed because they did not believe hard enough — which makes faith a
  currency and suffering a verdict. Nothing in Scripture supports that: Paul
  had extraordinary faith and was refused healing three times. Faith is also
  not the opposite of thinking or of doubting. Hebrews 11 lists people who
  argued with God, and Mark 9:24 preserves a prayer that admits unbelief and is
  answered anyway. Finally, "faith" in Scripture is not confidence in a desired
  outcome — it is trust in a person, which is why it can survive the outcome
  going the other way.
faqs:
  - q: What is the biblical definition of faith?
    a: >-
      Hebrews 11:1 defines it as confidence in what is hoped for and assurance
      about what is not seen. The definition builds uncertainty in rather than
      excluding it — the chapter goes on to list people commended for faith who
      died without receiving what they were promised.
  - q: Do faith and works contradict each other in the Bible?
    a: >-
      They address different questions. Paul argues in Ephesians 2:8-9 that
      salvation is not earned by works, answering how a person is put right
      with God. James argues in 2:17 that faith producing nothing is dead,
      answering how genuine faith can be recognised. Both reject a faith that
      is only a claim.
  - q: Is it a sin to doubt?
    a: >-
      Scripture does not treat it that way. Mark 9:24 records a man confessing
      belief and unbelief in the same sentence and receiving what he asked for,
      Thomas is invited to inspect the evidence rather than rebuked out of hand,
      and the Psalms are full of hard questions. Doubt directed at God tends to
      be treated as a form of engagement.

Hebrews 11 is usually read as a hall of fame. Read to the end it is stranger
than that — the chapter closes by noting that none of the people listed
received what they were promised during their lives.

That ending is what the definition at the top is for. Faith in this chapter is
not what you have when things work out. It is what the people who did not see
it work out had.
