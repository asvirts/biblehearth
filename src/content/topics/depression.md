shortAnswer: >-
  The Bible records despair in the lives of faithful people. Elijah asks to
  die, Job curses the day he was born, Paul says he despaired of life itself,
  and Psalm 88 ends in darkness. When Elijah collapses, God's first response is
  food and sleep. That practical care gives this passage lasting value for
  people living with depression.

misuse: >-
  Scripture doesn't describe depression as a sin or proof of weak faith. Using
  these verses that way can cause real harm. A prophet, an apostle, and several
  psalmists speak openly about despair without being rebuked for it. The command
  "Rejoice in the Lord always" isn't a demand to feel happy on command. Quoting it at
  someone during a depressive episode can resemble Job's friends, whom God
  later corrects for speaking wrongly. These passages also give no reason to
  avoid medical care. Depression can have physical and neurological causes,
  and God's care for Elijah begins with food, sleep, and time. Anyone in crisis
  should contact a doctor or crisis service. Getting help isn't a failure of
  faith, and no one should have to manage a crisis alone.
faqs:

    a: >-
      No. Scripture records deep despair in Elijah, Job, Jeremiah, David and
      Paul without calling it sin or a failure of faith. Psalm 88 even ends in
      darkness with no resolution. Across these passages, people in despair
      receive honest space, presence, and practical care.
  - q: What Bible character struggled with depression?
    a: >-
      Elijah is the clearest account. In 1 Kings 19, immediately after a major
      victory, he asks God to take his life and lies down under a tree. God's
      response is to let him sleep and feed him twice before any conversation
      begins. The account treats his physical needs as part of caring for him.

First Kings 19 responds to Elijah's despair with strikingly practical care. He
has just asked to die, yet God doesn't argue with him or bring up the miracle
of the previous day.

Elijah sleeps, eats, sleeps again, and eats again because the journey is too
much for him. Conversation comes later. The sequence recognizes that a person
in deep distress may need rest, nourishment, and patient company before words
can help.
