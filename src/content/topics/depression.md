shortAnswer: >-
  Scripture does not treat despair as faithlessness. Elijah asks to die, Job
  curses the day he was born, Paul says he despaired of life itself, and
  Psalm 88 ends in darkness with no resolution. What God does for Elijah first
  is not correct his theology but feed him and let him sleep — which is a more
  useful pattern than most of what gets quoted at people who are struggling.

misuse: >-
  Depression is not treated in Scripture as a sin or as evidence of weak faith,
  and using these verses that way causes real harm. The people quoted here
  include a prophet, an apostle, and the psalmists — and none of them is
  rebuked for despairing. "Rejoice in the Lord always" is not an instruction to
  feel happy on command, and quoting it at someone in a depressive episode is
  closer to Job's friends, who were told at the end that they had spoken
  wrongly. Nothing here should be read as a reason to avoid medical care.
  Depression has physical and neurological components, and Scripture's own
  pattern with Elijah begins with food, sleep and time. If you are in crisis,
  please contact a doctor or a crisis line; that is not a failure of faith, and
  these passages do not ask you to manage alone.
faqs:
  - q: Does the Bible say depression is a sin?
    a: >-
      No. Scripture records deep despair in Elijah, Job, Jeremiah, David and
      Paul without treating it as sin or as a failure of faith. Psalm 88 ends
      in darkness with no resolution and was preserved anyway. The consistent
      response in these texts is presence and provision rather than rebuke.
  - q: What Bible character struggled with depression?
    a: >-
      Elijah is the clearest account. In 1 Kings 19, immediately after a major
      victory, he asks God to take his life and lies down under a tree. God's
      response is to let him sleep and to feed him twice before any conversation
      happens — the physical need is addressed first, and the text presents this
      as care rather than indulgence.

The most striking thing about 1 Kings 19 is what is absent. Elijah asks to die,
and God does not argue with him, correct him, or remind him of the miracle he
performed the previous day.

He is allowed to sleep. Then he is fed. Then he sleeps again, and is fed again,
because the journey is too much for him. Only after that does anyone say
anything. It is a strangely practical passage, and a better model than most of
what is offered in its place.
