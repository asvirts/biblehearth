shortAnswer: >-
  The imagery Scripture uses for protection is shelter and refuge — a tower, a
  shadow, a shield — rather than immunity. That distinction is not a
  technicality: Satan quotes Psalm 91 to Jesus as a guarantee of physical
  safety, and Jesus refuses that reading outright. What is promised throughout
  is presence in the danger, and a God who is present with people who suffer.

misuse: >-
  Psalm 91 is the clearest case of misuse in Scripture, because Scripture
  itself contains the correction. Satan quotes it to Jesus as proof he can
  throw himself from the temple safely, and Jesus refuses, calling it putting
  God to the test. Read as a guarantee against accident, illness or violence,
  the psalm sets people up to conclude that God failed them or that their faith
  was insufficient — and it has been used exactly that way after tragedies. The
  same Bible records Stephen stoned, James executed, Paul beaten repeatedly, and
  Jesus crucified. Protection language in Scripture is real, but it describes
  refuge and ultimate safety, not exemption from harm. Trusting God is also not
  a reason to ignore ordinary precaution; Proverbs praises the prudent person
  who sees danger and takes cover.
faqs:

      Jesus as a guarantee that angels will prevent him being hurt, and Jesus
      refuses the reading, calling it testing God. The psalm uses shelter and
      refuge imagery — somewhere to go in danger — rather than promising
      immunity. Faithful people throughout Scripture were harmed.
  - q: What does the Bible say when you are afraid for your safety?
    a: >-
      Isaiah 43:2 promises presence when you pass through water and fire rather
      than a route around them, and Psalm 23:4 locates comfort inside the
      valley. Psalm 46:1 calls God an ever-present help in trouble, which
      assumes the trouble. The consistent shape is accompaniment through danger
      rather than its removal.

There is a rare thing in this topic: Scripture correcting a misuse of itself.
Psalm 91 gets quoted to Jesus as a promise of physical invulnerability, and he
declines it.

That exchange should govern how the rest is read. The protection language is
genuine, and it is shelter language — a tower to run into, a shadow to sit in,
company through the water. None of it is a fence around the property.
