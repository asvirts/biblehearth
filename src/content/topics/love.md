shortAnswer: >-
  The New Testament's central word for love, agape, describes commitment shown
  through action. First Corinthians 13 describes this love with patient,
  truthful, and enduring behavior, and Jesus commands it even toward enemies.
  Scripture grounds that demanding love in what God has already given: "We love
  because he first loved us."

misuse: >-
  1 Corinthians 13 is read at weddings so often that its actual setting gets
  lost. Paul wrote it to a church quarreling over whose spiritual gifts mattered
  most, so its words challenge a divided community. Love isn't identical to
  approval or silence. Romans 12:9 joins sincere love with hating evil, and
  Jesus sometimes spoke bluntly to people he loved. Love also isn't
  a reason to stay in danger. "Love covers a multitude of sins" describes
  patience with offenses; it must never be used to conceal abuse or silence
  someone who needs protection.
faqs:
  - q: What is the greatest commandment in the Bible?
    a: >-
      In Matthew 22:37-39 Jesus names loving God with all your heart, soul and
      mind as the greatest, then immediately adds love for your neighbor as
      yourself. He says all the Law and Prophets hang on these two commands,
      joining devotion to God with care for people.
  - q: What are the different Greek words for love in the Bible?
    a: >-
      The New Testament mainly uses agape, committed self-giving love, and
      philia, the love of friendship. Eros, romantic love, does not appear in
      the New Testament, and storge, family affection, appears only in compound
      forms. Agape is the word used in 1 Corinthians 13 and in the command to
      love enemies, which is why it can be commanded.
  - q: What does "God is love" mean?
    a: >-
      First John 4:8 describes God's nature, and the same letter grounds human
      love in God's prior action. The sentence doesn't reverse into "love is
      God" or make every claim labeled love divine. John remains deeply
      concerned with truth and with love demonstrated through action.

The word doing most of the work in these passages is *agape*. The New Testament
commands it, extends it to enemies, and describes it through concrete actions.
Its meaning reaches well beyond affection.

Feelings may come and go, but patience, truthfulness, and a willingness to stop
keeping score can still be practiced. That practical shape is what makes love a
meaningful command.
