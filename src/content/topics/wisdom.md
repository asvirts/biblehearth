shortAnswer: >-
  Wisdom in Scripture is practical skill at living rather than accumulated
  information, which is why Proverbs spends its time on speech, money, work and
  friendship. It begins with the fear of the Lord — a right-sized sense of who
  God is — and James says plainly that anyone lacking it should simply ask.
  Notably, the same books commend counsel, planning and careful judgement.

misuse: >-
  Proverbs 3:5 is regularly used to discourage thinking, which cannot be right
  in a book that praises counsel, planning, evidence and craftsmanship for
  thirty-one chapters. "Lean not on your own understanding" warns against
  treating your judgement as final, not against using it. Similarly,
  1 Corinthians 1:25 is quoted to shut down hard questions; Paul is contrasting
  the cross with the status-seeking wisdom of his culture, not recommending
  ignorance. The other common error is treating wisdom as a substitute for
  expertise. Scripture nowhere suggests that praying about a decision replaces
  seeking competent advice — Proverbs says the opposite, repeatedly, and locates
  safety in many advisers.
faqs:
  - q: What does "the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom" mean?
    a: >-
      The word covers awe and reverence rather than terror — taking God
      seriously enough that the assessment reorders your other priorities.
      Proverbs 9:10 calls it the beginning, a starting point you keep returning
      to rather than a stage you complete. The same books that commend it
      repeatedly tell people not to be afraid.
  - q: How do I know God's will for a decision?
    a: >-
      Scripture's most direct answer is James 1:5 — ask for wisdom, and it will
      be given generously without reproach. Beyond that it points to means
      rather than signs: Proverbs 11:14 and 15:22 locate safety in seeking many
      advisers, and James 3:17 gives a behavioural test for whether advice is
      wise. There is markedly less emphasis on signs than popular practice
      suggests.

Proverbs is not a book of spiritual abstractions. It is about how you speak,
what you sign, who you spend time with, whether you finish things. Wisdom there
is skill at living, and it is learnable.

Which is what makes the misuse of Proverbs 3:5 so odd. A book that spends
thirty-one chapters urging careful judgement is unlikely to be telling you, in
one verse, to stop using it.
