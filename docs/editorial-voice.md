# BibleHearth editorial voice

BibleHearth is for adults and older readers who are still getting oriented to
the Bible. The writing should sound like a knowledgeable friend sitting beside
the reader: clear, calm, specific, and comfortable saying when a passage is
difficult or disputed.

Use US English throughout BibleHearth's own writing.

## The voice in one paragraph

Explain the text before interpreting its importance. Use familiar words, but do
not make the ideas vague. Vary short and medium-length sentences. Speak directly
to the reader only when it makes an explanation easier to follow. Give the
positive meaning first; use a contrast or correction when the contrast itself
matters. The result should feel like prose written for a person, not a sequence
of thesis statements written for a search engine.

## What good BibleHearth copy does

- Answers the reader's question early, in ordinary language.
- Names the speaker, audience, setting, or argument when that context changes
  the meaning.
- Defines church vocabulary the first time it appears.
- Uses concrete details from the passage instead of broad religious slogans.
- Preserves uncertainty. If several readings remain plausible, explain why.
- Keeps pastoral, medical, legal, and crisis boundaries direct and practical.
- Uses a natural mix of sentences, paragraphs, lists, and questions.

## Habits to avoid

Several constructions are useful once and mechanical when repeated. Watch for:

- paragraph after paragraph built as "not X, but Y";
- constant use of "rather than," "actually," "genuinely," "the point," or
  "the honest reading" to make a sentence sound authoritative;
- an em dash in nearly every paragraph;
- headings that sound like compressed debate claims;
- telling the reader that a nuance is important instead of showing why;
- describing the site as brave, honest, kind, or unique;
- writing about featured snippets, retrieval models, or ranking inside the
  prose itself.

These are review signals, not forbidden words. Keep one when it is the clearest
way to say something. Rewrite it when the same rhythm has already appeared
nearby.

## Before and after

### Lead with what the passage says

Before:

> The comfort offered is not rescue from the valley but company inside it.

After:

> The valley is still there. David finds comfort in knowing he does not walk
> through it alone.

### Explain without scolding

Before:

> Scripture treats anxiety less as a sin to be scolded and more as a weight to
> be handed over.

After:

> When the Bible speaks about anxiety, it usually gives worried people
> something to do: pray, ask for help, and remember that God is near. It does
> not shame them for feeling afraid.

### Let a detail carry the insight

Before:

> The shift also moves the psalm from surviving to being celebrated, which is a
> deliberate escalation.

After:

> The first picture keeps David alive. The second welcomes him to a full table.
> By the end of the psalm, he is doing more than surviving.

### Use uncertainty plainly

Before:

> Most careful readers hold one of these with the other two in view, and that is
> not a failure of nerve — it is what four verses of evidence will support.

After:

> Each reading explains part of the passage, and each leaves questions behind.
> Four verses do not give us enough evidence to settle every one of them.

## Page-specific notes

### Chapter studies

Start the short answer with what happens in the chapter. Let the outline follow
the chapter's movement. Theme headings can be simple if the explanation beneath
them is specific. A caution section should correct a real misreading, not invent
an argument so the page can sound discerning.

### Topics

Readers often arrive with a real concern, not an abstract interest. Begin with
what the selected passages consistently offer. Keep verse glosses close to the
verse's setting. Medical and crisis language must remain clear and must never
suggest that prayer replaces professional care.

### Answers

State the short answer first. Present competing readings in the terms their
supporters would recognize, then give the strongest objection. Preserve the
difference between evidence, inference, tradition, and uncertainty.

### Version guides and utility pages

Help the reader make a decision or finish a task. Do not turn every paragraph
into an argument. Legal, privacy, licensing, attribution, and support language
must keep the same factual promises after an edit.

### Spanish and Brazilian Portuguese

Write naturally in the language of the page. Do not translate English sentence
structure word for word. Preserve references, claims, uncertainty, and the
site's existing locale conventions. These pages currently receive model
self-review; do not describe them as human-reviewed.

## Final read

Before a page is done, read it aloud and ask:

1. Does the first paragraph answer the question without warming up?
2. Do several nearby paragraphs use the same contrast or punctuation pattern?
3. Is every interpretive claim still supported by the same evidence as before?
4. Could a newcomer understand every religious term?
5. Did any rewrite change a verse reference, factual claim, safety instruction,
   license, or degree of certainty?
6. Does this sound like one person explaining something to another?
